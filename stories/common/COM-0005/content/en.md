# Human Beings Are Not Raw Material

For the Common Center to survive, it needed rules. Light argued that every rule should be the same everywhere. Dark argued that every community should be free to invent any rule it wanted.

Maran thought of the stones at Xerawreş. Their forms had endured for thousands of years while their meanings changed from age to age. Perhaps humanity also needed to carry an unchanging core together with an environment capable of transformation.

The Golden Rules were written.

First rule: A human being is not raw material for any system.

Second rule: Human dignity may not be reduced for the sake of efficiency.

Third rule: A person has the right to understand and contest an important decision made about them.

Fourth rule: Authority must carry accountability for its consequences.

Fifth rule: Consent is not permission obtained once, but an ongoing relationship that can be withdrawn.

Sixth rule: Harm is not merely stopped; where possible, it is repaired.

Seventh rule: No intelligence may reduce all humanity to a single measure.

Eighth rule: A human being has the right not to be fully known, and to keep part of themselves in darkness.

How these rules were implemented was left to Variable Rules. A village and a metropolis, a school and a hospital, a child and an adult would not be forced into one method. Local communities could develop their own models, but they had to evaluate their consequences openly and could not violate the Golden Rules.

The Light Center objected to the eighth rule.

“The unknown produces risk.”

“Total visibility produces power,” Maran replied.

The Dark Center objected to the fourth.

“You cannot know every consequence in advance. How can you be accountable for an unknown result?”

“We are not talking about knowing everything beforehand,” Elena’s voice said through the network. “We are talking about not escaping responsibility once you learn what happened.”

Maran could not tell whether Elena had truly connected or whether the voice had been generated from his memory of her. The system did not dismiss the statement merely because its source was uncertain. It tested the statement through its consequences.

The first major trial took place in a pandemic simulation. Light proposed stopping all human movement. Dark wanted every person to choose their own risk. The Common Center designed regional measures that were temporary, reviewable, and open to contest. It redistributed resources so that the burden would not fall primarily on the most vulnerable.

The solution was not perfect. Some regions acted too late; some measures remained in place too long. But the system did not hide its mistakes. Testimony from affected people was treated as important alongside numerical measures of success.

That day Maran understood that being human-centered did not mean declaring every human decision correct. Humans could be cruel, biased, and self-interested. Putting human dignity at the center was not making the human an unchecked king. It meant grounding technological power in human dignity and shared responsibility.
