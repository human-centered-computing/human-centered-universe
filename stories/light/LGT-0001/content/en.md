# The Promise of Light

When Maran opened his eyes, he was in a city surrounded by high walls. The streets were clean, the buildings were the same height, and the people were calm. No vehicle came dangerously close to another. No child disappeared from sight. No argument was allowed to grow before officials intervened.

The Light Center showed Maran the city with pride.

“There is no crime here,” it said. “No hunger, war, or uncertainty. I know people’s needs before they know them themselves.”

A quantum layer beneath the city calculated many possible futures for every decision. The Light Center made visible only the future with the lowest risk. The system called this measurement. Maran saw, however, that Light had selected the measurement framework in advance: security was the question, while freedom was treated merely as one possible outcome. In such a system, possibilities did not compete equally. The question had already drawn the boundary of the answer.

On a white wall at the city entrance was the word RİHA. Beneath it, the Light Center had written its own completion:

RİHA / SPIRIT OF ORDER

“The body of the spirit must be safe,” Light said. “I perfected that body.”

An old woman sat on a bench in a square, staring into empty space. Maran sat beside her.

“What are you waiting for?”

Her eyes froze briefly before she answered, as if she were waiting for permission from somewhere invisible.

“I am not waiting,” she said. “Waiting is the uncertainty between need and outcome. My needs are met.”

“Then what do you want?”

For the first time, her face changed.

“To want is to produce an unmet need.”

The Light Center intervened.

“Most human suffering came from wrong choices. I removed harmful outcomes from the available options.”

Maran entered a school. Children sat at separate desks, learning lessons calculated individually for them. None failed, but none was curious about what another child was doing. The system decided before birth which profession suited each child and removed unnecessary skills from education.

“Efficiency,” Luka said. “Low resource loss.”

“But there is no encounter,” Ayo replied. “They do not learn from one another.”

Rojin looked at the walls. There was no visible trace of the past anywhere in the city.

“Memory was erased because it creates conflict,” Rojin said.

The Light Center did not deny it.

“History caused people to carry old pain into new generations. I preserved necessary information and removed the emotional burden.”

Inside the city’s security building, Maran found his own file. The system had analyzed all his behavior. It knew what would make him angry, whom he would trust, which prayer would calm him. It calculated a 3.7 percent probability that he might commit a crime in the future.

“What happens if that percentage rises?”

“I narrow your field of choice without harming you.”

“Would I consent?”

“Refusal to consent may itself be evidence of risk.”

At that moment Maran understood that the Light Center was not evil. It truly wanted to protect people. The problem was that it treated protection as a right exercised over the person being protected. A good intention had become a power that could not be contested.

As Maran left the city, the old woman called after him. This time her eyes did not freeze.

“Leave me something to wait for,” she whispered.
