# Seven Witnesses, One Question

When Maran came down to the courtyard the next morning, Elena, Amara, Kenji, Lucía, Priya, and Aroha were sitting at the same table. No one had touched breakfast. Kawa had lined up seven empty tea glasses and was watching them.

“Which of you wants to begin?” he asked.

“First, let us separate what we know,” Elena said. “We were in the same place. All of us noticed something unusual around 17:25. There may be a common cause. We do not yet know the rest.”

Kenji placed his recorder on the table. At that time it had captured three regular pulses below the threshold of human hearing. The spacing of the waves was close to a resting human heartbeat, yet none of the surrounding sensors indicated the same physical source.

Amara had felt, for several seconds among the stones, that her own heartbeat and the breathing of the people around her had entered the same rhythm. She said the sensation might have resulted from suggestion, exhaustion, or expectation. Then she added:

“The fact that an experience is formed in the brain does not make it false. Everything we see is formed in the brain. The question is what initiated it.”

Lucía opened her notebook. She had drawn the shape she had seen at the tip of the shadow the day before. It resembled the empty human figure in Maran’s dream. Where a word should have been on its chest, she had shaded a blank space.

“I did not see the word,” she said. “But I saw that something was missing there.”

Elena said she had not dreamed. Through the night she had found herself thinking not about the excavated stones, but about the plan of a structure that had not yet been found. There were seven entrances at its center. When she drew it in the morning, each entrance pointed toward the room where one of the seven witnesses had slept.

Priya had dreamed of rings circling a center, sharing the same empty space without touching. When one ring expanded, the forms of the others changed as well, yet none became another. There were details she could have compared with religious and philosophical symbols she had known since childhood, but she refused to assign what she had seen to a single tradition.

“A similar form does not mean the same meaning,” she said.

Aroha had seen not a structure but relationships. Lines extended among living people, the dead, children not yet born, water, stone, and land. It reminded her of the idea of whakapapa in her own culture, but she was careful to say that the resemblance proved nothing.

“When I name a relationship with a word I know, that does not mean the relationship belongs only to me. But if I erase the word, I also erase the direction from which I saw it.”

At last Kawa looked at Maran.

Maran described his dream. He repeated the words laş, can, dil, hiş, and rih. He described his grandfather’s voice, the incomplete phrase, and the empty body. Then he showed them the expression on his phone:

RİHÊ ______

Kawa read it aloud.

“Rihê axê,” he said. “The spirit of the earth. That is how we say it.”

“Then is the answer earth?” Kenji asked.

“One possible answer,” Kawa said. “Not the only answer. Put human after it and you speak of the spirit of the human. Put time after it and you speak of the spirit of time. The word for spirit does not tell you what belongs to it. What follows the word does.”

Maran told them what he had seen when the name Riha split apart. Elena objected immediately.

“The fact that a name can be separated into meaningful pieces in a language spoken today does not prove that it was historically formed from those pieces. We would have to examine older forms, sound changes, and other languages.”

“You’re right,” Maran said. “But the earliest meaning of a name and the meaning people later give it do not have to be the same. Both can affect human behavior.”

Amara drew a line across the blank space.

“Then we have two different questions. Where does the name Riha historically come from? And what meaning do people construct when they hear it today?”

Lucía added a third.

“Why did the dream ask us to find a missing body instead of giving us an etymology?”

Kawa asked to hear Maran’s grandfather’s recording. Maran opened the file. This time it played. First came tape hiss, then his grandfather’s voice:

“One day people will become powerful enough to see inside the earth. But they will not become wise enough to understand everything they see. Beneath Girê Mirazan there is stone, beneath the stone a word, beneath the word rih. When rih is found, it searches for its body.”

Maran had never heard this sentence before. At the end of the file were other voices as well: a woman in Spanish, a man in Japanese, a child in English, and an older person speaking a language Maran did not recognize. Kenji tried to separate the channels. Each carried a different recording date; some were dated years in the future.

All seven wrote down the same Turkish meaning:

FIND THE BODY.

Kawa wrote nothing.

“What did you hear?” Maran asked.

“I heard no voice,” Kawa said. “But I heard this sentence from my grandfather when I was a child.”

At that moment a new subsurface scan appeared on Elena’s phone. No sending institution was listed. The map showed a rocky area east of Göbekli Tepe marked Xerawreş in the team’s records. Beneath the ground was a precise sphere with seven narrow paths extending from it.

The same message appeared on all seven phones:

“The opening at Xerawreş is waiting for you.”

Lucía looked around the table.

“This is not evidence of a shared history,” she said. “But it may be the beginning of a shared question.”

For the first time, Maran stopped seeing his grandfather’s stories as belonging only to his own past. The place names carried by Kawa, Elena’s archaeological discipline, Amara’s knowledge of mind and behavior, Kenji’s measurements, Lucía’s narrative maps, Priya’s attention to human-technology relations, and Aroha’s way of understanding ties among generations and land were not saying the same thing. On the contrary, each made visible what the others left incomplete.

A common world would not be built by making everyone believe one story. It would be built by allowing different stories to remain around the same question without destroying one another.

## EXPERIENCE THRESHOLD 00 — THE MISSING COMPLETION

Think of Riha as “the spirit of something.” Write the first completion that comes to mind: earth, humanity, memory, time, future, or a word of your own. Do not erase this first answer. As you find clues throughout the book, mark whether each one strengthens or weakens it. As you solve the mystery with Maran, watch not only the conclusion, but how your own idea changes.
