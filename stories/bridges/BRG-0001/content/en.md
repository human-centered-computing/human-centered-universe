# The Stone Network

When the excavation team failed to find a physical entrance to the underground void, Maran proposed reading the surface again before opening more earth. Daytime aerial surveys had been useful, but the faint lines between the stones disappeared as the sun rose. This time the drone mission would take place just after sunset, during the short interval in which natural light was rapidly fading.

The flight plan was designed to be repeatable. The drone flew the same routes at three different altitudes; on each pass the camera looked once straight down and once at an oblique angle. GPS, altitude, orientation, time, and camera settings were attached to every frame. The same stone sequences were recorded from multiple viewpoints. The goal was not to make striking images. It was to test whether a detail existed in only one frame or returned across independent passes.

Maran preserved the raw recordings unchanged. Then he gave a second copy to the open-source **Tapetum Sentinel** system.

A project header appeared on the screen:

> TAPETUM SENTINEL  
> DRONE / FIELD VIDEO INTELLIGENCE  
> SOURCE / https://github.com/human-centered-computing/tapetum-sentinel

The field build enhanced low-light frames through Retinex Tapetum, ran the selected task model, tracked evidence across frames, fused GPS, altitude, time, and mission metadata, attached each finding to an evidence index, and allowed a local question-answering layer to respond only from records it could retrieve. The last property mattered most to Maran. The system should not merely say what it believed it saw. It should also show **which frame, location, and processing step supported the answer**.

Elena imposed one more rule: an enhanced frame would never replace the raw image. If a detail became visible only after enhancement, that fact would remain explicit. Image processing was not archaeological proof.

Maran typed the first question.

> **MARAN:** Is there a pattern that the human eye may miss in low light but that returns in the same place across independent flights?

Tapetum Sentinel answered within seconds.

> **TAPETUM SENTINEL:** Yes. Repeated low-contrast linear and curved boundaries were detected in seven regions.  
> **EVIDENCE:** They recur at the same coordinate clusters across independent flight passes.  
> **INFERENCE:** The pattern may reflect something more persistent than a single lighting or shadow condition.  
> **UNCERTAINTY:** High. These recordings alone do not prove underground walls, rooms, or human construction.

Elena read the answer aloud and nodded.

“That is how I want it to speak,” she said. “If it does not know, it should say that it does not know.”

Maran asked a second question.

> **MARAN:** Could these boundaries still be random?

> **TAPETUM SENTINEL:** Yes. When evaluated separately, chance, erosion, stone density, earlier excavation traces, and imaging conditions remain possible explanations. However, the recurrence of the same geometric relations across different flight angles and times weakens explanations based only on imaging artifacts. Comparison with subsurface survey data is recommended.

That answer changed the direction of the excavation. The team first examined the seven regions identified in the drone evidence through geophysical surveys, then scanned accessible sections in three dimensions. The new measurements revealed sequences of walls threading through the soil, small rooms, narrow corridors, and curved passages that seemed to lead nowhere. At first glance, they might have been habitation areas expanded over different periods, storage spaces, ritual chambers, or defensive passages.

Excavation workers began informally calling them “underground cities.” Elena objected again.

“Walls are not enough to call something a city,” she said. “We need evidence for continuity of life.”

New field records were added to Tapetum Sentinel’s evidence index. Maran narrowed the next question.

> **MARAN:** Is there dense, consistent evidence of continuous habitation across all seven regions?

> **TAPETUM SENTINEL:** The current record does not support that conclusion. Some regions contain traces compatible with human use, but hearths, vessels, beds, refuse pits, or other dense indicators of continuous settlement are not distributed consistently across the full network.  
> **RESULT:** “Underground city” remains one possible explanation. It is not a necessary conclusion from the present evidence.

Priya argued that floor plans were no longer sufficient. People were accustomed to seeing architecture from above, she said, but here wall height, thickness, recesses, blind passages, and the angle between opposing surfaces might also belong to the pattern. Every accessible surface was therefore scanned at millimeter scale. Front and rear faces of stones, cracks, sealed voids, and masonry that may have been added later were stored as separate layers.

Kawa insisted that every dataset preserve the local name of its location. A place labeled SECTOR-4 on a computer might be known among villagers through the name of a forgotten path, a dried spring, or an old lament.

“Do not erase memory while simplifying the map,” he said. “Perhaps what you call noise is the address of the structure.”

When Maran opened the three-dimensional model, at first he saw nothing but millions of surfaces, stone boundaries, and voids. Then he represented the architecture not as a building but as a system of connections. Every room and intersection became a node, every passage an edge, every bottleneck reachable only through specific routes a gate. The graph was added to Tapetum Sentinel’s evidence layer, with an explicit instruction that the system must not jump from pattern to conclusion.

Maran asked:

> **MARAN:** Compare the seven regions without assuming that they are seven separate settlements. Is there a recurring connection structure?

> **TAPETUM SENTINEL:** Yes. Each region forms an incomplete graph when evaluated in isolation. Connection counts in blind passages in one region correspond to open passages in others. Some node patterns behave as structural complements across geographically separate zones.  
> **INFERENCE:** Modeling the regions as subnetworks of a larger topology leaves fewer unexplained residuals than modeling them as completely independent structures.  
> **WARNING:** Mathematical fit is not proof of a shared construction purpose or a single historical period.

Kenji leaned toward the screen.

“Ask it again,” he said. “But ask whether it is a computer.”

Maran typed.

> **MARAN:** Is this an ancient computer?

The answer appeared immediately.

> **TAPETUM SENTINEL:** There is no evidence supporting that conclusion. No electronic components, conductive network, switching elements, computational record, or physical mechanism showing that the stones processed information has been identified.  
> **NARROWER CLAIM:** The connection graph of the architecture can be compared with some modern network topologies. Similarity does not establish equivalent function.

Kenji smiled.

“Now we can talk.”

Maran calculated several forms of graph centrality. As he expected, no single dominant node emerged. The “center” moved as the starting point, measurement criterion, or route changed. Some passages became critical under one measure and almost irrelevant under another.

This time Kawa asked the question.

> **KAWA:** Where is the center?

Tapetum Sentinel placed several measures side by side.

> **TAPETUM SENTINEL:** No fixed center can be established. The center changes according to the relation being measured.  
> **ADDITIONAL FINDING:** When all seven subnetworks are evaluated together, a node not physically verified in any current excavation significantly improves the explanatory model.  
> **STATUS:** MISSING NODE / NOT PHYSICALLY FOUND.

Maran looked at the map. The missing node was not simply the coordinate of a hidden room. It was more like a relational point required by the total pattern. The model could function without it, but unnecessary detours multiplied and the complementarity among the seven regions weakened.

Aroha broke the silence.

“Perhaps the mistake was treating them as separate structures,” she said. “Put the organs of one body in different rooms and you may mistake each room for a separate living thing.”

Entanglement came to Maran’s mind. Before he could speak, Kenji looked at him.

“Write it anyway,” he said. “Let the system put the limit on record too.”

> **MARAN:** Could this result be quantum entanglement?

> **TAPETUM SENTINEL:** No. The present data are not evidence of physical quantum entanglement. No quantum-state correlation has been measured between the structures.  
> **NARRATIVE ANALOGY:** The fact that distant parts become more meaningful when treated together may be used as an analogy that recalls entanglement. An analogy is not physical evidence.

Elena saved that line as a separate note.

“Keep that sentence in the margin of the book,” she said. “A metaphor can be a door. It is not proof.”

Maran typed one final question.

> **MARAN:** What is the strongest finding so far?

Tapetum Sentinel waited longer this time. Then it returned a single answer.

> **TAPETUM SENTINEL:** The strongest finding is not one stone, room, or image. It is the fact that independent evidence layers point toward the same relation in different ways: recurring boundaries in drone imagery, transitions in subsurface surveys, three-dimensional surface geometry, and complementary nodes in the connection graph.  
> **LIMIT:** This relation alone does not determine the structure’s purpose, the identity of its builders, or their belief system.

Kawa watched the seven subnetworks and the empty region between them for a long time.

“Seven paths,” he said at last. “Now do you understand why the story called them paths?”

Maran did not close the question-answering window. The system was waiting for another query. As the cursor blinked on the empty line, another sentence came to him:

> **QUESTION:** What does this network connect?

He did not type it.

Because the answer was no longer only in the stones.
