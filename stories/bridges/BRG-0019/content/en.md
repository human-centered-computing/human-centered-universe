# Ministry of Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation

## The First Local Experiment

Some institutions are first created by law.

Others exist for years inside a person's mind before they are given a name.

Maran's imagined institution had no building, no minister, no official organization chart and no budget.

But it had a purpose:

**Technology should not govern human life. Technology should improve it.**

Over the years, Maran gave this future model a name:

# Ministry of Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation

It did not yet exist as a ministry.

It was a research question, a future institutional model and a long-standing idea.

Maran believed that such a structure should not begin with a national organization chart in Ankara. Before becoming a national proposal, it had to meet real people, real institutions, real constraints and real failures.

The first step would therefore not be a ministry building.

It would be **Harran University**.

---

# From Ankara to Harran University

Maran had spent many years in Ankara, working on informatics projects at Hacettepe University and pursuing graduate studies in different fields.

There he learned the strengths of central institutional work: specialization, access to expertise, planning, reporting, coordination and organizational memory.

Later, working locally, he encountered another reality.

A problem that looked small from the center could become a major obstacle in the field. Human resources were more limited. A single person might have to carry responsibilities from several specialties. Some tasks continued only because they had always been done that way. Some people worked hard without ever seeing what their effort had changed.

Maran began to take notes.

A repeated process.

A delayed decision.

A good idea lost between ranks.

A student blocked by bureaucracy.

A software developer rebuilding the same function for the third time.

A project that failed but taught something important.

Over time, those notes converged on one question:

> **Does the institution work for the human being, or does the human being work for the institution?**

---

# The Harran School: A Question from the Past

The name Harran opened another door in Maran's thinking.

The region carried the memory of the historic Harran School, remembered as one of the old centers of learning in the region.

Maran did not claim direct institutional continuity between the historic school and today's Harran University.

The connection he saw was symbolic:

**knowledge gathered, was discussed, and then traveled beyond its place of origin.**

The tools were different now.

Computers.

Artificial intelligence.

Open-source software.

Distributed work.

Data.

Global networks.

But the central question remained:

**What is knowledge for?**

Could a solution produced locally improve the life of someone far away?

Could a mistake made in one institution prevent another institution from repeating it?

Could contribution remain visible even when titles and managers changed?

---

# The First Structure

When Maran came to Harran University, he had established an Informatics Coordination Office but had not activated it.

Over time he concluded that simply creating another IT unit would not be enough.

A new name with an old working culture would not be transformation.

So he redesigned the structure and prepared to activate it under a new name:

# Coordination Office for Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation

Software would still be built.

Artificial intelligence would still be used.

Processes would still be digitized.

Data would still be analyzed.

But every project would have to answer another question:

# What does this change in human life?

The coordination office would become the first real local experiment for the larger model Maran had imagined for years.

---

# A Laboratory Before a Ministry

The goal was not to create a small ministry inside Harran University.

The coordination office did not possess such authority.

Instead, Harran University could become a research and implementation laboratory.

New work models could be tested there.

Human-AI collaboration could be tested there.

Open-source production could be tested there.

The visibility of contribution beyond office and rank could be tested there.

Remote and hybrid work could be evaluated there where lawful and practical.

The limits of evaluating work by outcome and human impact, rather than only by time spent at a desk, could be explored there.

Most importantly:

**failure would not be hidden.**

Every project would ask:

What worked?

What failed?

Why did it fail?

Who benefited?

Who was negatively affected?

What time was saved?

What new cost was created?

What changed in a human life?

What should the next team do differently?

The accumulated experience would become a memory that could move from Harran University to Sanliurfa, from Sanliurfa to Türkiye, and eventually to wider global collaboration.

---

# NextLife

The digital expression of this model was called **NextLife**.

NextLife was not designed as a single application.

It was conceived as a shared ecosystem for human-centered informatics.

NextID handled identity.

NextOrg represented institutional structure.

NextRole represented authorization and responsibility.

NextVoice carried human feedback.

NextAI supported analysis.

NextProject preserved project memory.

NextCode and NextDeveloper supported software and developer collaboration.

Yet Maran believed the most important layer was not an application.

It was:

# Contribution

A project could involve many people, but years later only the names of officeholders might remain.

The person who first identified the problem could disappear from the story.

The developer could disappear.

The field worker could disappear.

The student whose idea solved the problem could disappear.

The new system therefore adopted a principle:

# Verified contribution should not be erased.

Managers could receive credit for real support.

Students could receive credit for real technical work.

Researchers could receive credit for ideas and analysis.

Field workers could receive credit for data and testing.

Rank alone would not create authorship.

The goal was not to diminish leadership.

The goal was to write each real contribution to the person who actually made it.

---

# HUMAN

The Human Center asked the first question:

> **What changed for the human being?**

A project might produce thousands of lines of code and still fail to improve anyone's life.

A system could be technically perfect and still create new burdens.

For this reason, every project needed a human-centered impact target before it began, and an honest comparison between expected and actual impact when it ended.

The Human Center also challenged time-based work measurement.

Legal working hours and institutional duties would remain.

But value could not be inferred only from physical presence.

Problem solving, verified contribution, learning, quality improvement and measurable benefit also mattered.

At the same time, the Human Center warned:

> **Do not turn every moment of human life into a performance metric.**

Learning has value even before it produces a measurable output.

Human development is not merely a productivity tool.

---

# LIGHT

The Light Center asked for evidence.

If the project claimed to improve human life, it had to show how.

How long did the process take before?

How long does it take now?

What changed in cost?

How many people were affected?

Did quality improve?

Did a new burden appear elsewhere?

Who made which contribution?

Who made which decision?

What did the AI recommend, and what did the authorized human decide?

Good intentions were not enough.

Claims had to be separated from evidence.

But Light also warned:

> **Transparency is not the destruction of privacy.**

Personal data, private life, institutional secrets, security information and critical infrastructure details could not simply be opened to everyone.

Visibility and protection had to coexist.

---

# DARK

The Dark Center looked at what did not yet exist.

Could a solution developed at Harran University be adapted elsewhere in Sanliurfa?

Could another university in Türkiye improve it?

Could a developer in another country contribute to it?

Could a local mistake become global learning?

The answer was open source where safe and appropriate.

Reusable software cores could be shared.

Others could inspect, adapt and improve them.

Contribution could remain visible.

But open source was not the same as opening everything.

Personal data, confidential information, institution-specific security configurations and critical-system details had to remain protected.

Dark also asked a harder question:

What happens when artificial intelligence becomes more capable than it is today?

Maran's answer could not depend on a specific model or technology.

The enduring rule had to remain human-centered:

**AI may be a work partner. It is not the final owner of responsibility.**

---

# The Three-Center Test

HUMAN asked:

**What changed for the human being?**

LIGHT asked:

**How do we know?**

DARK asked:

**What else is possible?**

Maran realized that no single center was sufficient.

Human concern without evidence could become good intention without proof.

Measurement without human meaning could reduce people to numbers.

Innovation without limits could weaken responsibility and safety.

Human-centered transformation emerged where the three centers constrained and improved one another.

---

# Sanliurfa, Türkiye, World

Harran University would be the first laboratory.

Sanliurfa would become the first wider field.

Real problems from education, agriculture, health, law, disaster management, environment, public services and working life could enter the system through human feedback.

NextAI could support pattern finding and analysis, but a single complaint would not automatically become a general truth.

Projects would be researched, prototyped, authorized where necessary, tested, measured and documented.

When enough real experience accumulated, the next layer would be Türkiye.

Transfer packages could contain technical architecture, human impact, costs, legal needs, risks, open-source components, maintenance requirements, failures and scaling conditions.

Only then would the larger Ministry Model become meaningful.

The logic was not:

Create a ministry first and then search for its purpose.

The logic was:

**test the need, method, limits, success and failure locally before proposing national scale.**

And beyond Türkiye, open-source collaboration could allow useful knowledge to travel farther still.

---

# The First Step

Maran looked again at the name he was about to activate at Harran University:

# Coordination Office for Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation

It might look like a small administrative change.

For him, it was the first real step of a model he had imagined for years.

There was no Ministry of Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation yet.

The model had not been proven correct.

Some ideas might fail.

That was precisely why the experiment mattered.

Real people.

Real projects.

Real outcomes.

Real mistakes.

Shared learning.

Maran wrote the first principles beneath the new name:

> **Human time is valuable.**  
> **Human contribution should remain visible.**  
> **Human development is itself a value.**  
> **Technology should work for human beings.**  
> **Failure is also part of learning.**

Then he added one final line:

> **Harran University is not the destination. It is the first prototype.**

And the experiment began.
