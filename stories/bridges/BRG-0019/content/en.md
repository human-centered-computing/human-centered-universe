# Ministry of Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation

## The First Local Initiative

The corridor at Harran University was still quiet that morning.

Maran stood before the door of the Informatics Coordination Office he had established years earlier but had never activated.

The name on the door was about to change.

For him, however, more than a few words were changing.

The new name would be:

# Coordination Office for Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation

Maran looked at the sign for a long time.

For the first time, he felt that a larger idea he had carried in his mind for years was touching the wall of a real institution.

That idea had a name:

# Ministry of Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation

But no such ministry existed yet.

There was no minister,

no central organization,

no budget,

and no nationwide authority granted to it.

There was only an idea,

years of accumulated experience,

and a question waiting to be answered:

> **Should technology be used to make institutions more powerful, or to make human life more meaningful, fairer, and easier?**

Maran had been pursuing the second answer for years.

But he no longer wanted only to think about it.

He wanted to test it.

With real people.

With real institutions.

With real mistakes.

With real results.

So the first step of the national structure he imagined would not be a ministry building in Ankara.

It would be **Harran University**.

---

# What He Learned in Ankara

Maran had lived in Ankara for many years.

At Hacettepe University he had worked on informatics projects, pursued graduate education in different fields, and observed from inside how a large institution operated.

A centralized working order had strengths.

There was specialization.

There were human resources.

There was institutional memory.

When a problem arose, it was often possible to reach someone who had encountered a similar problem before.

Planning, reporting, and coordination were more visible.

Maran learned the value of this discipline.

Then he began working locally.

And he encountered another reality.

He saw that some problems that looked small at the center could have very large consequences locally.

Human resources were more limited.

One person might have to cover several different fields of expertise at the same time.

Some work continued simply because it had always been done that way.

The same information was entered again and again,

the same document was recreated in different places,

and units unaware of one another repeated similar work.

Some employees put in great effort but could not see the result of their labor.

Some good ideas disappeared between offices and ranks.

The distance between the person who saw a problem and the person authorized to solve it could become very long.

Maran did not try to solve everything immediately.

First he took notes.

A problem.

A repetition.

A delay.

An employee complaint.

A successful practice.

A failed project.

A difficulty experienced by a student.

A process a manager was trying to fix.

A developer forced to do the same work again.

Over time the notes connected to one another.

And eventually they all began to point to the same question:

> **Was the institution working for the human being, or was the human being working for the institution?**

---

# The Harran School: A Question from the Past

The name of the university where Maran worked opened another door in his mind.

The geography of Harran carries the memory of the **Harran School**, remembered as one of history’s old centers of science and higher learning.

Maran did not claim direct institutional continuity between the historic Harran School and today’s Harran University.

What interested him was another connection:

**Knowledge being produced somewhere, debated, verified, and then carried into a wider world.**

In the past, the tools were books, observations, debates, and students.

Today the tools were different:

computers,

artificial intelligence,

open-source software,

data,

distributed work,

and global networks.

But the question remained the same:

> **What is knowledge produced for?**

Maran’s answer was simple:

**It gains meaning to the extent that it can improve human life.**

So Harran University would not merely be an implementation site.

It would be the first laboratory in which knowledge was tested again.

---

# A Laboratory Before a Ministry

Maran’s aim was not to build a small ministry inside Harran University.

The Coordination Office had no such authority either.

But Harran University could become a **research, development, and implementation laboratory**.

New working models could be tested there.

New digital systems could be developed there.

The division of responsibility between humans and artificial intelligence could be examined there.

Open-source production methods could be applied there.

Ways of making contribution visible independently of rank could be tested there.

The benefits and limits of remote and hybrid work could be measured there.

And the limits of evaluating performance not only by working hours but by results, development, and human impact could be observed there.

Failures would not be hidden either.

At the end of every initiative, the same questions would be asked:

What worked?

What did not work?

Why did it not work?

Who benefited?

Who was harmed or disadvantaged?

How much time was saved?

What costs arose?

Did anything truly change in people’s lives?

What should be done differently next time?

And could this model be applied somewhere else?

The answers would not remain at Harran University.

They would become a memory of experimentation that could first be used by Şanlıurfa,

then Türkiye,

and eventually the world.

The digital counterpart of this idea had a name:

# Next-Life

---

# Next-Life: One for the Institution, Personal for Every User

Maran did not think of Next-Life as a single application.

He wanted the different services a person needed in institutional digital life to meet in one common ecosystem.

Today an employee might use one system for identity,

another for documents,

another for projects,

another for meetings,

another for tasks,

another for learning,

and another for feedback.

The systems often did not recognize one another.

The same person was redefined in every application.

The basic idea of Next-Life was to reverse this:

> **The human being would not wander among applications; the applications would come together around the person within the workspace they were authorized to use.**

To explain this, Maran and his team imagined a working day.

An employee enters the system in the morning,

contributes to a project in the afternoon,

reports a problem,

prepares a document,

joins a meeting,

completes a training,

and at the end of the day can see the result of the work performed.

This journey explains why Next-Life has thirty applications.

---

# First Layer: The Human Enters the System

When the employee opens the computer, **Next-ID** appears first.

The task of Next-ID is to verify who the person is.

User accounts,

sessions,

multi-factor authentication,

single sign-on,

and, when needed, external identity providers are managed there.

But Next-ID answers only one question:

**“Who is this person?”**

Another application knows the person’s place within the institution:

**Next-Org.**

Next-Org is the organizational memory of the institution.

What units exist?

Which unit reports to which?

What is a person’s position?

Which administrative or academic structure do they belong to?

Who is acting in another person’s place?

Next-Org holds this context.

Then **Next-Role** enters.

Next-Role answers:

**“What may this person do here?”**

Can the person only view a document?

Can they edit it?

Can they approve it?

Are they merely a participant in a project,

or the project manager?

Do they have temporary authority?

Has an authority been delegated to someone else?

Without confusing title with authority, Next-Role limits permissions according to duty and context.

Maran insisted on another distinction.

**Next-Privacy** is not an authorization system.

Next-Privacy manages a person’s privacy and data preferences.

Which communication channel do they prefer?

What level of visibility do they allow?

What analytical uses do they consent to?

What are their personal preferences?

Next-Role determines authority.

Next-Privacy carries privacy preferences.

There is also **Next-Config**, which determines how the system behaves.

Next-Config manages institutional or user configuration,

module settings,

whether features are enabled or disabled,

and dynamic system options.

It is not an authorization application either.

Thus, when a person enters the system, five different questions have been answered:

Who?

Where?

What may they do?

What are their privacy preferences?

How should the system be configured for them?

The personal workspace where all these come together is **Next-Life**.

Next-Life shows the user their digital institutional life under a single roof.

Tasks,

documents,

projects,

meetings,

approvals,

learning,

payments,

and other services meaningful to that person come together there.

The institution uses one platform,

but everyone sees a different workspace according to role and need.

**One for the institution, personal for every user.**

---

# Second Layer: Artificial Intelligence Does Not Decide; It Helps People Decide

Inside Next-Life there is a shared intelligence layer:

**Next-AI.**

Next-AI can summarize text,

classify reports,

extract important entities from documents,

suggest draft responses,

flag risks,

make search results easier to understand,

group similar problems,

and propose possible solution paths for a project.

But Maran drew an invisible boundary around the application:

> **Next-AI is not the decision-making authority.**

Artificial intelligence suggests,

shows sources,

explains uncertainty,

and makes alternatives visible.

The binding decision belongs to the authorized human being.

Because producing information and carrying responsibility are not the same thing.

It is also not enough for an AI output to be approved by a human without being understood.

Saying “a human signed it” does not necessarily mean a human truly made the decision.

Therefore the user must be able to reject the AI recommendation,

change it,

and explain why a different decision was made.

When the information produced by Next-AI must become visible, **Next-View** enters.

Next-View presents reports,

dashboards,

performance indicators,

charts,

matrices,

and project or process status in forms people can understand.

For that reason, there is no need for a separate **Next-Report** application.

Reporting and visualization belong inside Next-View.

Maran wanted not more applications,

but clear responsibilities without overlap.

---

# Third Layer: How Does Work Move?

An institution is not made only of people and documents.

Work has a beginning,

steps,

responsible people,

decisions,

and an end.

**Next-Flow** manages this movement.

Next-Flow defines which steps a process follows,

where it moves under which condition,

which rule runs when,

waiting times,

service levels,

and to whom work is routed.

Thus a process stops being a set of invisible rules buried inside software code.

People can see how the work proceeds.

When a decision is needed, **Next-Approval** enters.

Next-Approval manages approval chains,

acceptance,

rejection,

returning work for revision,

decision records,

and signature status.

Maran did not create a separate electronic-signature application.

The thing being signed is a document,

while the decision is part of an approval process.

Therefore, when an electronic signature is needed, **Next-Document + Next-Approval** work together.

When work must be assigned to a person or unit, **Next-Task** is used.

Next-Task shows the responsible person,

deadline,

priority,

subtasks,

task status,

and the process or project to which the task belongs.

A larger, time-bounded body of work is managed in **Next-Project**.

Next-Project is not merely a task list.

It keeps together the project goal,

milestones,

work packages,

resources,

risks,

deliverables,

stakeholders,

and contributions made throughout the project.

Because projects cannot run without people talking to one another, there is **Next-Meeting**.

Next-Meeting records the agenda,

participants,

minutes,

decisions,

and resulting actions.

When people say “let’s do this” in a meeting, the words do not remain in the air.

If necessary they become a Next-Task assignment,

a Next-Approval decision,

or a Next-Project action.

The time dimension is managed by **Next-Calendar**.

Personal calendars,

institutional calendars,

project calendars,

meetings,

appointments,

deadlines,

and planned work can be seen on the same timeline.

For Maran, saying “there is work” was not enough.

The flow,

decision,

responsible person,

project,

meeting,

and time of that work had to be connected.

---

# Fourth Layer: The Institution Speaks and People Make Their Voices Heard

Maran did not want Next-Life to become a management system that worked only from the top down.

People also had to be able to add data,

ideas,

complaints,

suggestions,

and decisions to the institution.

For this there were four different applications, and they were not interchangeable.

**Next-Collect** is the survey and form collection system.

When research is conducted,

structured information is requested from a defined group,

field data is collected,

or results are analyzed, Next-Collect is used.

But Next-Collect is not an election system.

For the things a person wants to tell the institution in everyday life, there is **Next-Voice**.

Suggestions,

complaints,

requests,

satisfaction,

wishes,

problems,

and needs can be expressed there.

The user does not need to know the correct institution or unit.

The human being describes the problem.

The original choice is recorded.

Artificial intelligence or the Coordination Office may separately assess which institutions may be related,

but the user’s first statement is not silently overwritten.

When institutional choice or voting is required, **Next-Vote** is used.

Candidates,

ballots,

votes,

results,

secret-ballot requirements,

eligible voters,

and auditable election records belong to this application.

A survey is Next-Collect.

Feedback is Next-Voice.

Election and voting are Next-Vote.

This distinction is deliberately protected.

The layer that delivers information to people is **Next-Message**.

Person-to-person or group messages,

files,

system notifications,

reminders,

process alerts,

and project notifications can be seen there.

External channels such as e-mail or SMS can be connected through Next-Connect when necessary.

Thus the institution stops being a structure that only collects data,

and becomes a structure that can speak with people and listen to them.

---

# Fifth Layer: Institutional Memory Does Not Disappear

An important part of an institution lives not in people’s heads,

but in its documents and data.

That is why **Next-Document** is the center of document and record management.

Documents,

folders,

metadata,

versions,

templates,

relationships,

access information,

archives,

and electronic records are kept there.

It can be visible who changed a document and when.

Large files,

media,

object storage,

backup,

and retention policies are managed by **Next-Storage**.

Next-Document understands the meaning and lifecycle of a document;

Next-Storage can securely store its physical digital content.

But when millions of records accumulate over the years, another problem emerges:

**Finding them.**

For that there is **Next-Search**.

Next-Search can perform central search over documents,

projects,

messages,

code,

support records,

and other authorized content.

But search does not mean “show everything to everyone.”

Next-Search must be authorization-aware.

The user should be able to find only results they are authorized to see.

Next-AI may assist search when needed,

but it may not cross access boundaries.

For structured management of raw data there is **Next-Data**.

What data sources exist?

Who owns a dataset?

How does data flow?

What transformations does it pass through?

What is its quality?

Who may access it?

What datasets have been prepared for analysis?

Next-Data becomes the common governance layer for the data catalog,

data pipelines,

ETL processes,

data quality,

and analytical datasets.

For Maran, an abundance of data was not the same as knowledge.

What mattered was knowing the source,

meaning,

quality,

and authority of the data.

---

# Sixth Layer: Who Builds the Software, and How Does the System Stay Alive?

Next-Life is not made only of the screens end users see.

Behind it is another world of developers,

integrations,

logs,

monitoring,

and support.

The gateway into this world is **Next-Developer**.

Next-Developer is the developer portal and technical guide.

APIs,

SDKs,

authorization examples,

starter applications,

integration guides,

architectural explanations,

and development policies are available there.

But Next-Developer is not a source-code repository.

For source code there is **Next-Code**.

Next-Code is the Git-based development space for institutional or otherwise non-public software projects.

Repositories,

branches,

commits,

pull requests,

code review,

merges,

webhooks,

and pipeline processes can run there.

A generalizable and safely shareable open-source core can be published in global open-source spaces such as **Human-Centered Computing**.

Institution-specific,

confidential,

or security-sensitive code may remain in Next-Code.

Maran’s purpose was not to rewrite every system from scratch.

Universities already had student,

personnel,

finance,

and other specialist systems.

Throwing them away and rebuilding everything would be a great waste.

So **Next-Connect** was designed.

Next-Connect is the integration and API gateway.

Through REST,

SOAP,

GraphQL,

webhooks,

events,

data transformations,

adapters,

and protocol conversions, it allows old and new systems to talk to one another.

Thus Next-Life can become not a giant replacement project,

but an upper ecosystem that connects existing systems when necessary.

To understand what has happened while a system runs, there is **Next-Log**.

Application logs,

audit trails,

error records,

request records,

security events,

and process records are kept there.

Next-Log tells what happened in the past.

**Next-Monitor** watches how the system is doing now.

Is the service up?

What is the response time?

Has the error rate increased?

Is resource use normal?

Is the service-level objective being met?

Should an alert be raised?

Next-Monitor answers these questions.

When a technical or user-originated problem occurs, **Next-Support** enters.

Help desk,

request records,

incident and problem management,

knowledge base,

service-level tracking,

and the support process from user feedback to resolution are kept there.

Maran saw these six applications as the invisible backbone of the system.

Because a good interface was not enough.

The system had to be developable,

connectable,

observable,

auditable,

and supportable.

---

# Seventh Layer: Service and Learning

A human-centered system should not only manage work.

It should also touch the real needs of a person inside the institution.

One of these is payment.

**Next-Pay** is the payment and collection integration layer.

Institutional fees,

collections,

POS,

virtual POS,

and connections to payment providers can be managed there.

But Next-Pay will not try to become a new bank or accounting system.

It will integrate with existing financial systems.

The other need is learning.

**Next-Learn** makes human development part of institutional work.

Courses,

programs,

content,

enrollment,

assignments,

assessment,

certification,

development tracking,

and, where necessary, integration with existing learning-management systems can be handled there.

This application mattered especially to Maran.

A person receiving education in an area where they are weak,

studying independently,

and acquiring a new capability should not be treated as “time when no work was done.”

Learning in the right place is also a contribution to future human and institutional capacity.

# The First Voice, the First Project

In the first days of the Coordination Office, an employee left a short entry in Next-Voice:

“We enter the same information in three different places again and again.”

In the old system, the sentence might have remained a complaint.

In Next-Life, the journey was only beginning.

Next-Voice received the record.

It did not alter the user’s original statement.

Next-AI analyzed similar records and the authorized information it could reach.

It did not automatically treat one complaint as the general truth of the institution.

It showed similar situations,

possible causes,

uncertainties,

and possible solution paths.

A human in the Coordination Office reviewed the report.

“Is this really a widespread problem?”

the person asked.

Structured data was collected from relevant employees through Next-Collect.

Next-Data showed the existing data sources.

Next-Search found earlier documents and support records about the same process.

Similar requests appeared in Next-Support.

The problem was no longer merely one person’s feeling,

but it was still a working issue that required human verification.

A project was opened in Next-Project.

Next-Org showed which units were involved.

Next-Role limited who could access which data and perform which actions.

Next-Task distributed assignments.

Next-Calendar scheduled meetings.

Next-Meeting recorded discussions, decisions, and actions.

Next-Document collected existing forms and procedures.

When a change required approval, Next-Approval was used.

A document requiring electronic signature entered the process through Next-Document and Next-Approval working together.

Next-Connect built the integration that would obtain the necessary data from existing institutional systems and reduce repeated data entry.

Next-Developer showed developers which APIs and rules to use.

In Next-Code it was recorded who wrote the code,

who reviewed it,

and in which version it changed.

Next-Log held errors from testing.

Next-Monitor watched the new service in operation.

Next-Message informed pilot users about the changes.

Next-Learn offered a short training to staff who would use the new process.

And at the end, Next-View showed a few indicators everyone could understand:

How much did processing time decrease?

How much did repeated data entry fall?

How many people were affected?

How did the error rate change?

Did user experience improve?

Technically, the system worked.

But the Human Center did not yet say “successful.”

Some users had difficulty understanding the new screen.

Some were worried that the measurements would be used to monitor them personally.

Some did not want to change old habits.

The project was reopened.

Next-Voice received new feedback.

Next-Learn changed the training.

Next-Privacy explained more clearly how visibility and preferences were managed.

Next-View showed people not only what managers saw,

but also the results of their own work.

A new version was prepared.

The old version was not erased.

Because failure was also part of institutional memory.

# The Memory of Labor

While working locally, Maran repeatedly encountered the same problem.

Many people could work on a project.

Yet years later, only the names of a few people holding office might be remembered.

The person who first noticed the problem could be forgotten.

The person who developed the idea could be forgotten.

The person who wrote the code could be forgotten.

The person who collected field data could be forgotten.

The person who prevented a wrong decision could be forgotten.

A young employee’s idea could eventually be told as if it had belonged to someone else.

So the new system had a basic principle:

# Verified labor must not be erased.

A contribution record should show not a person’s office,

but the work actually performed.

Idea.

Analysis.

Code.

Testing.

Documentation.

Field work.

Mentoring.

Project management.

Institutional support.

Maintenance.

Each should be visible separately.

Even when a manager changes, past contribution should not disappear.

If there is an error, the old record should not be silently rewritten;

a new correction record should be appended.

Because Maran was seeking more than transparency.

He was seeking **justice in institutional memory**.

---

# Not Hours, but Impact on Human Life

One subject Maran emphasized most was working time.

Working hours,

duties,

and responsibilities required by law and regulation would continue.

But measuring a person’s value only by how many hours they sat at a desk would not be human-centered.

The new approach also had to see other things:

What problem did the person solve?

What output did they produce?

Whose life changed, and how?

How much time did they save?

Did quality improve?

Did they contribute to team learning?

Did they develop themselves?

Did they receive training in an area of weakness?

Did they gain a new capability?

But there was a dangerous boundary here.

A human-centered system could turn into a surveillance system that scored every movement of a person.

So Maran wrote another principle:

> **What is measured should not be the human being, but the result of the work and the verifiable contribution.**

The value of a human being could never be reduced to a performance indicator.

Measurement should remain only a tool for understanding the work and the system better.

---

# Human Center

When the Human Center stood before Next-Life, its first question was:

> **What changed for the human being?**

It did not care how many applications had been built.

It did not ask how many lines of code had been written.

It did not accept the number of meetings as success.

It asked:

Did a person wait less?

Were they freed from unnecessary work?

Could they see the result of their labor?

Could they access the service more easily?

Did they experience a fairer process?

Did they learn better?

Could they develop themselves?

Did they find their work more meaningful?

Then it warned:

> **Do not reduce the human being to data. A human being is more than what you can measure.**

For the Human Center, the purpose of the system was not merely to increase productivity.

It was to protect a person’s time,

dignity,

agency,

development,

and right to meaningful work.

---

# Light Center

The Light Center asked a different question:

> **How do we know this?**

You say people’s lives improved.

What is your evidence?

How long did a process take before?

How long does it take now?

Did the error rate change?

Did cost rise or fall?

Who contributed?

Who made which decision?

What source did the artificial intelligence use for a recommendation?

Where is the uncertainty?

Were negative results recorded?

The Light Center saw the value of traceability in systems such as Next-ID,

Next-Role,

Next-Log,

Next-Document,

Next-Project,

Next-Code,

and Next-View.

But then it filled the screens with personal data.

And it warned:

> **Transparency is not the destruction of privacy.**

Not everything should be open to everyone.

Personal data,

institutional secrets,

security information,

and critical-infrastructure information had to be protected.

The purpose of the Light Center was not to make everything visible,

but to make **the right thing visible to the right person in a verifiable way**.

---

# Dark Center

The Dark Center’s question was different again:

> **What else is possible?**

Could a solution developed at Harran University be used at another university?

Could a problem in Şanlıurfa be solved by a developer elsewhere in the world?

Could a student contribute to an open-source project and affect the lives of thousands of people?

Could a failure report from one project prevent another institution from making the same mistake?

The Dark Center was the space of freedom,

experimentation,

uncertainty,

new possibilities,

and transformation.

But it too warned:

> **Experiment without boundaries is not transformation; it is lack of control.**

Open source did not mean that every piece of data should be open.

Artificial intelligence should not replace human responsibility.

Experimentation should not erase legal and institutional boundaries of authority.

A new working model should not free people on paper while making them work more invisibly.

The Dark Center was necessary for change.

But it needed the Light Center’s evidence

and the Human Center’s boundary.

---

# Three Centers at the Same Table

One day all three centers looked at the same project.

The Human Center asked:

“What changed for the human being?”

The Light Center asked:

“How do we know?”

The Dark Center asked:

“What better thing could we do instead?”

At that moment Maran understood more clearly that the three centers were not rivals.

If the Human Center stood alone, good intentions could remain without evidence.

If the Light Center stood alone, measurement and order could come before the human being.

If the Dark Center stood alone, transformation could become uncontrolled experimentation.

Together they formed another balance:

> **Without the human being, there is no purpose.**
>
> **Without light, there is no verification.**
>
> **Without darkness, there is no transformation.**

# From Harran University to Şanlıurfa

Harran University was the first laboratory.

But Maran did not want to stop after preparing a successful demo there.

A model that works only in a controlled environment has not yet been tested in real life.

The second ring would be Şanlıurfa.

Education.

Agriculture.

Health.

Law.

Disaster management.

Environment.

Public services.

Transportation.

Working life.

Real problems could arrive through Next-Voice,

be analyzed by Next-AI together with sources and uncertainties,

be evaluated by people,

be turned into projects through Next-Project,

be tested in the field,

have their human impact measured through Next-View,

and have the results openly reported.

Failure would be recorded just as success was.

Because a failed experiment,

if reported correctly,

could become the beginning of successful work somewhere else.

# From Şanlıurfa to Türkiye

When enough real experiments accumulated, the model could move into the third ring:

Türkiye.

But in Maran’s vision, a future Ministry of Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation would not be a center trying to produce every solution in Ankara.

On the contrary,

it should be a coordination and transformation model capable of seeing problems emerging locally,

verifying solutions produced locally,

carrying useful models elsewhere,

and allowing institutions to learn from one another.

If a solution was developed at Harran University,

tested in Şanlıurfa,

and its success and failure were measured,

another place in Türkiye might not have to start from zero.

A national transfer dossier could be prepared:

What was the problem?

What was the solution?

What was the technical architecture?

What legal and regulatory boundaries existed?

What did it cost?

What was the impact on people?

What were the risks?

Which open-source components could be reused?

Where did it fail?

What would need to change for use somewhere else?

Thus the center could exist not to control the local level,

but **to multiply verified knowledge produced locally**.

---

# From Türkiye to the World, and from the World Back to the Local

The fourth ring was the world.

Generalizable and safe software components could be shared as open source.

Developers in other countries could inspect the code,

find errors,

develop new features,

adapt it to the needs of their own societies,

and return their contributions to the common structure.

Contribution should not disappear.

Wherever a person lived, their verifiable contribution should remain visible.

But the path was not one-way.

A solution leaving Harran University for the world could improve through global contributions and return to Şanlıurfa again.

Transformation could therefore become not an order distributed from a center to the periphery,

but **a learning network continuously circulating between the local and the global**.

---

# The Real Question of the Ministry Model

After years of thinking about the structure, Maran could finally see more clearly what it needed to become.

The main question was not:

“Should a new ministry be established?”

Other questions had to be answered first:

Is this model really needed?

Does the prototype at Harran University work?

Does it improve life for the people doing the work?

Can it solve real problems in Şanlıurfa?

Can other institutions use it?

Does the open-source approach increase reuse?

Does recording contribution actually improve employee motivation?

Does impact-oriented work improve efficiency while making people feel watched?

Does artificial intelligence support human decision-making,

or does it quietly begin to replace it?

Are failures really reported?

A large structure established before these questions are answered might merely produce a new bureaucracy.

That is why the work at Harran University was not small.

**It had to be real enough to show that the big idea itself might be wrong.**

---

# The First Step

Maran returned to the door of the Coordination Office.

The silence of the morning was gone.

Inside, people were talking,

projects were opening on screens,

code was being written,

feedback was being reviewed,

some ideas were accepted,

others rejected,

some projects failed,

and some were restarted.

Maran looked once more at the sign:

**Coordination Office for Informatics and Human-Centered Transformation.**

It was not an ending.

It was not a ministry.

It was not even a claim.

**It was a prototype.**

And the task of a prototype was not to prove itself right,

but **to reveal reality**.

Under it, he wrote four sentences:

> **Human time is valuable.**
>
> **Human labor should be visible.**
>
> **Human development is also contribution.**
>
> **Technology should work for people.**

Then he added a fifth:

> **If we are wrong, we will make that visible too.**

Because the first work at Harran University was not merely trying to produce a successful model.

**It was trying to build a system that learns in order to reach the right model.**

# The Observer’s Choice

If you were designing this system, which center would you strengthen first?

## Human Center — The Meaning of Labor

A person should be able to see what their work changes in other people’s lives; their time, dignity, development, learning, and invisible contributions should also be treated as valuable.

**Human Center +6 · Light Center +2 · Dark Center +2**

## Light Center — Evidence of Labor

Contribution, results, authority, and decisions should be verifiable; real labor should remain visible independently of rank, and failure should be recorded just as success is.

**Human Center +2 · Light Center +6 · Dark Center +2**

## Dark Center — Freedom of Transformation

Models tested locally should be shared through open source and allowed to be adapted, improved, and extended into new possibilities by others.

**Human Center +2 · Light Center +2 · Dark Center +6**

Between the three choices, one question remained unresolved:

> **How can a human-centered system measure labor without measuring the human being?**

Perhaps this was one of the most important questions the experiment at Harran University had to answer.
