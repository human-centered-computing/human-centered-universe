# Riha: A Name Is a Door

Winter in Berlin put people on the same trains while keeping them far apart. When Maran left his home in Neukölln in the mornings, he spoke German with a baker, Arabic with the neighbor next door, English with colleagues; when his mother called, he shifted into Turkish, and when he listened to his grandfather’s old recordings, he returned to the half-finished Kurdish of his childhood. By the end of the day, he often no longer knew which language he was thinking in.

Eight years earlier, he had come to Europe to pursue a PhD in Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence and settled in Berlin. His doctoral research asked how AI systems that affected people’s lives could be made more understandable, contestable, and fair. Along the way he had worked with teams on urban technologies in Rotterdam, accessible public spaces in Barcelona, disaster communication in Nairobi, and AI ethics in Montreal. The people he worked with, befriended, and loved came from different countries, beliefs, and ways of life. He had grown used to hearing five languages around one table. He had learned not to infer a person’s homeland from a passport, belief from a name, or pain from the language in which it was spoken.

He described himself as a citizen of the world. Sometimes the phrase meant freedom; sometimes it was simply a polished way of saying that he did not fully belong anywhere.

The most concrete center of his life in Berlin was his two children. Seven-year-old Murat wanted to understand how every working device functioned and always found a second question for whatever he had just learned. Three-year-old Rodi often added a single word after the answers she heard: "Why?" Four years separated them; even when they looked at the same room, they saw the world from different heights and with different kinds of curiosity. Maran thought that every system he had helped build in different countries eventually led back to the world these two children would inherit. He did not want to leave them his truths. He wanted to leave them a world in which they could test those truths.

Maran was a Human-Centered Transformation Architect. He combined his background in computer engineering with the research approach he had developed through his doctorate in human-centered AI, working on how relationships among technology, people, institutions, law, culture, and the environment could be redesigned. His work was not merely about producing better models. He examined who a technology changed, whom it empowered, whom it rendered invisible, and how it altered people’s ability to decide for themselves. Years earlier, he had built models so that machines could know people better. Eventually he realized that the deeper question was not how well a machine knew a person, but how much power it gained over that person once it believed it knew them.

One day, while he was joining a meeting in Stockholm remotely, the face of an old man appeared on the screen. The image lasted only a second. No one else in the meeting noticed. When Maran rewound the recording, the presentation was all that remained. Yet in his headphones he heard a voice his grandfather had recorded years ago:

“Guh bide axê, lawê min. Ax bêdeng e, lê ji bîr nake.”

Maran remembered the sentence from childhood: “Listen to the earth, my son. The earth is silent, but it does not forget.”

After his grandfather died, Maran had digitized the remaining tapes, though he had never listened to all of them. He understood some of the stories; for others he had to look up words in dictionaries. His grandfather sometimes called the city Urfa, sometimes Riha. When speaking of the hills, he used names absent from official maps: Girê Mirazan, Xirabreşk, Newala Çorî.

As a child, Maran had once asked what Riha meant.

“Nav bersiv nîne. Nav derî ye,” his grandfather had said. A name is not an answer. A name is a door.

“What is behind the door?”

“The one who hears the name will find it.”

At the time, Maran had taken it for an old man’s fondness for riddles. Now the creation date of the last unopened audio file on his computer had changed by itself. The system claimed the file had been created twelve thousand years earlier. Such a date was meaningless to the operating system. Maran copied the file, stripped its metadata, and examined it on three different devices. Each time, the date returned.

Two weeks later he flew from Berlin to Urfa. He called the trip neither research nor a return home. It was simply a visit he had postponed for too long. On the drive from the airport, he watched stone houses appear between concrete blocks and old paths vanish beneath new roads. He tried to compare the city with the one he remembered from childhood, then realized that his memory had changed as much as the city.

The next morning he joined a small group heading to Göbekli Tepe. Their guide, Kawa Baran, did not introduce himself as a local historian.

“I collect names,” he said. “Historians write their dates. I record what people call a place when they are going there.”

Kawa pointed toward the hill.

“On the sign, this is Göbekli Tepe. Some call it Girê Mirazan: the hill of wishes. Some remember the name Xirabreşk: the black ruin. If the same place has two names, one of them does not have to be wrong. A person can climb the same hill with hope and descend from it with fear.”

Maran had not expected to hear from someone else the names preserved on his grandfather’s tapes.

The group also included Elena Rossi, an archaeologist born in Bologna; Amara Okafor, a Lagos-born neuroscientist working in London; Kenji Sato, an acoustic engineer from Kyoto; Lucía Álvarez, who designed interactive narratives in Bogotá; Priya Nair, a human-computer interaction researcher born in Kerala; and Aroha Te Rangi, a Māori systems ecologist from Aotearoa/New Zealand. They had met one another that morning. Elena attended to stone and stratigraphy, Amara to human behavior, Kenji to unusual acoustic reflections, Lucía to the way narratives multiplied, Priya to the relationship between technology, belief, and everyday meaning, and Aroha to the ties among people, land, generations, and responsibility.

Maran told none of them about his grandfather’s recording.

As he walked among the T-shaped stones, he first looked at the animal reliefs. Snakes, foxes, birds, and forms he could not name did not seem to emerge from the stone; they seemed to be drawn back into it. Although the structures were open to the sky, Maran had the impression that they continued beneath the ground.

At 04:17, the sounds of the site suddenly seemed to move away. Tourists were still talking, footsteps still struck the ground, and the wind had not stopped, yet none of it seemed to reach him. The shadow of one stone stretched in the opposite direction from the sun. At its tip was a thin line resembling his grandfather’s walking stick.

“Did you see it?” Lucía asked.

Maran turned toward her.

“See what?”

Lucía closed her notebook without answering. Kenji was staring at the screen of his recorder. Elena announced that visiting hours were ending. Amara noticed the color had drained from Maran’s face and asked whether he was all right.

“I’m just tired,” he said.

That night he slept in the stone house Kawa had recommended. He left the window open. From a distance came dogs, water dripping in the courtyard, and the faint hum of his phone while it charged. He tried to open his grandfather’s final recording, but the file would not play. Only one expression appeared on the screen:

RİHÊ ______

Maran fell asleep while staring at the blank.

In his dream, he was not at Göbekli Tepe. Yet he recognized every stone around him. There was no sky. The earth had lost the distinction between above and below. His grandfather stood among the stones, but his face would not remain fixed. At moments he became the man Maran had known in childhood; then a young woman he had never seen; then people from other continents.

His grandfather placed a hand on Maran’s chest.

“Laş,” he said.

Maran felt the weight of his body.

“Can.”

His heart began to beat faster.

“Dil.”

Love, fear, anger, and longing rose inside him at once.

“Hiş.”

He became aware that he was dreaming.

His grandfather whispered the final word:

“Rih.”

Maran felt that something inside his body was looking back at him. It was not his memories, because it also carried things he had forgotten. It was not his faith, because it remained present inside his doubts. It was not his nationality, because he could see a fragment of himself in every face before him.

The surfaces of the stones opened. Maran saw people in different ages and geographies gathered around fires. Some buried their dead. Some named newborn children. Some watched the sky. Some carved into stone the shape of an animal they feared. Their languages were different. None knew the stories of the others. Yet all of them wanted not to lose, to be protected, to give meaning, and to leave a trace for those who would come after them.

The voices multiplied. Each spoke a different language. Maran could not understand them one by one, yet somehow he knew they were asking the same question:

“To which body does the soul belong?”

An empty human figure formed among the stones. First it resembled Maran, then Elena, Amara, Kenji, Lucía, Priya, Aroha, and people he had never known. A single word appeared on its chest:

RİHA

The word split in two.

RİH / A

The final letter opened like a door. Where another word should have been, there was darkness.

Maran turned to his grandfather.

“Riha is the soul of what?”

His grandfather did not answer. He pointed to one of the stones. On it was not a map of the world, but human hands touching one another. No hand resembled another; no hand completed the form alone.

“To share is not to become the same,” his grandfather said. “You will not live the same pain. But you can learn to recognize one another’s pain as real.”

A child’s voice rose from the darkness:

“Find the body. The soul has found you.”

Maran woke at 03:17. A thin black dust lay in his palm. He thought it might have rubbed off when he touched the stones during the day. Then he remembered that touching the stones was forbidden and that throughout the visit he had kept his hands in his pockets.

There was a new email on his phone. It had no sender address. The subject line contained only a time: 04:17.

The message was shorter still:

“The opening at Xerawreş is waiting for you. Purify yourself and return to your essence.”

Maran did not delete it.
