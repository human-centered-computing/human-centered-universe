# Memories from the House of Work

## The First Day

I do not remember who first opened the door for me. I remember only that there was a place waiting inside, and that someone pointed to it without asking whether I wanted it.

“Sit here,” he said.

I sat.

There were people everywhere, yet almost nobody spoke. They moved with the confidence of those who had repeated the same movements so often that the body had learned them before the mind.

I thought I had come to do something.

I had ideas. I believed that if a place was arranged by human beings, human beings could arrange it differently.

One of the older ones looked at me and smiled.

“You will understand,” he said.

I thought he meant that I would understand the work.

I did not know that he meant I would understand the place.

## The Record

Every arrival had to be recorded.

I asked why.

“So that they know you came.”

“But I am here.”

“That is not the same thing.”

I found the answer strange. A person's presence seemed obvious to me. Yet the mark beside a name mattered more than the person standing before it.

One day I forgot to leave the mark.

Nothing I had done that day disappeared. The work was there. The people had seen me. The result was still on the table.

Still, something was wrong.

They asked why my name was missing.

I explained.

They listened politely.

Then they asked me to correct the record.

That was when I began to understand that a system does not always measure what exists. Sometimes it measures what it has decided must be visible.

## The Rules

There were rules everywhere.

Some were written. Others were known only by watching what happened to people who broke them.

I once asked why one of the rules existed.

The man beside me thought for a while.

“It has always been here.”

“That is not a reason.”

He looked at me as though I had said something unnecessarily complicated.

“It is the reason we have.”

After that I stopped asking.

Not because I had accepted the rule, but because I had discovered something more powerful than a rule: the habit of not asking about it.

## The One Who Was Called the Supervisor

He was not always harsh.

That made him harder to understand.

Sometimes he protected someone from an unnecessary demand. Sometimes he refused a good suggestion without explanation. Sometimes he listened carefully and then said nothing.

People watched his face before they spoke.

I noticed that what he could make visible was almost as important as what he could command.

A person's work could remain unnoticed for months.

Then, with a few words from him, the same work could become important.

I began to understand why people did not merely want his approval.

They wanted to exist in the version of the house that he could see.

## Whose Idea Was It?

There was a man who disliked unnecessary repetition.

He had found a simple way to make a difficult task easier. He explained it to the supervisor.

The supervisor listened.

“That is interesting,” he said.

Nothing happened.

Then, at another gathering, the supervisor presented the same idea.

“I have been thinking,” he began.

The room listened.

The method was adopted. People praised the improvement.

The man who had created it sat quietly.

Later I asked him why he did not say anything.

He shrugged.

“What would I say?”

“That it was your idea.”

He smiled without amusement.

“It was my idea before it became useful.”

I understood then that an idea can be taken without anyone touching the person who made it.

The worst part was not losing the credit.

It was learning not to offer the next idea.

## The Work Nobody Saw

There was a woman whose name rarely appeared anywhere.

Yet when something was missing, she noticed it.

When a mistake was left behind, she corrected it.

When someone needed help, she was usually the person who had already understood the problem.

Her work was not counted.

One day she was absent.

The place continued to function, but badly.

People searched for the missing pieces.

Only then did they discover how much of the house depended on work that had never been recorded.

Someone said, “We should include this in the report.”

Another answered, “How do we measure it?”

No one knew.

The work had been real.

The measurement had not.

## What Could Be Counted

A new system arrived.

Everything would be measured.

At first, people welcomed it.

At last, they said, effort would be visible.

Soon everyone learned which actions produced numbers.

They began choosing those actions.

Work that helped people but produced no number was postponed.

Work that produced a number but solved little was completed quickly.

The tables looked better.

The house looked busier.

People congratulated one another.

I could not explain why the place seemed less alive.

## The Hidden Failure

One person became famous for speed.

He completed more tasks than anyone else.

His numbers were excellent.

But the same problems returned.

He had learned to finish things, not necessarily to solve them.

Nobody asked why the work came back.

The system asked only how many had been closed.

A person can satisfy a measure while failing the purpose behind it.

That was the strange lesson.

The better the number became, the easier it was to stop looking at what the number could not see.

## The New One

A new person arrived.

He asked questions.

Why this rule?

Why this form?

Why this order?

Why does this have to be repeated?

At first people laughed kindly.

Later they became uncomfortable.

Eventually someone told him:

“Do not make things difficult.”

He did not understand.

He had thought questions made things clearer.

Then I watched him change.

He began giving the answers he had once rejected.

When another newcomer asked why something was done, he said:

“This is how it is done here.”

I recognized his voice.

It was the voice of the man who had told me that I would understand.

## The Silent Ones

There were rooms where everyone seemed to have an opinion.

Nobody spoke.

I once asked someone why.

“We spoke before.”

“And?”

“Nothing happened.”

That answer was enough.

Silence did not always come from fear.

Sometimes it came from repeated evidence that speech changed nothing.

After a while, silence became efficient.

It required no explanation.

It left no trace.

It could not be measured as resistance.

## Do No More Than Asked

One man had always done more than required.

He stayed when others left. He solved problems that were not assigned to him. He carried responsibility without being asked.

Then one day he stopped.

He did exactly what was written.

Nothing more.

People noticed.

“You used to do more.”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

He looked at the work in front of him.

“I stopped knowing who it was for.”

No one answered.

The work was still completed.

The number did not fall much.

Only something invisible had disappeared.

## The Owner of Success

When something failed, people searched downward.

Someone had made a mistake.

Someone had missed a step.

Someone had not followed the rule.

When something succeeded, the story moved upward.

The people who had done the work became part of the background.

The person who announced the success became its face.

No rule required this.

It simply happened often enough to become natural.

I began to see that responsibility had a direction.

Failure traveled downward.

Recognition traveled upward.

## The Nameless

There were people whose work was everywhere and whose names were nowhere.

They repaired what others broke.

They explained what others misunderstood.

They carried the unfinished parts of other people's work.

When a list of successful people was published, their names were absent.

I asked one of them whether that bothered him.

He said:

“You get used to it.”

I had heard those words before.

I wondered how many things a person could become used to before he stopped noticing that something had been taken from him.

## The Two Sides of the Door

There was a door.

Those inside wanted to go out.

Those outside wanted to come in.

One day I stood beside it and watched both groups.

The people outside imagined freedom.

The people inside imagined relief.

When the door opened, people changed places.

For a moment, everyone seemed satisfied.

Then the movements began again.

I could no longer tell which side was inside.

## What Was It For?

I remember the people.

The rule-bound ones.

The ones who questioned.

The ones who stopped questioning.

The ones whose ideas disappeared into other people's names.

The ones whose work was never counted.

The ones who learned to produce numbers.

The ones who learned to survive by becoming silent.

I remember the door most clearly.

And I remember the question that none of us asked often enough.

What was all this for?

I never found the answer.

Perhaps that was the question we had been avoiding from the beginning.

Perhaps the house had never needed us to understand its purpose.

Perhaps it needed only that we continue to enter.

And perhaps the strangest thing was this:

I could no longer tell whether I had entered the house,

or whether the house had entered me.
