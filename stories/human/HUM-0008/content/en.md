# The Thirteenth Notch

Just before sunrise, a new mark appeared on the twelfth pillar of Girê Miraza.

Twelve notches stood side by side on the stone. Beneath them was a thirteenth line, still unfinished…

When Maran emerged from the darkness, the pillars trembled faintly.

“These marks were not here yesterday,” said the observer.

“Perhaps yesterday you had not yet asked the question that would let you see them.”

The observer moved a finger toward the half-finished line. A voice rose from within the stone:

**TWELVE PEOPLE REGISTERED.  
FOOD ALLOCATED TO TWELVE PEOPLE.  
THIRTEENTH PERSON NOT FOUND.**

“Who is missing?” the observer asked.

Maran looked at the pillar.

“We do not know whether they are missing. The system never counted them.”

The twelfth pillar split open with light.

The darkness at the center widened into a circle.

**FIRST VIBRATION**

The observer did not fall from the stone chamber. Time changed position around them.

## Before numbers

On a cold night, the observer saw a woman sitting across from a fire.

She was scoring lines into a bone with a sharp stone. Some stood alone; others formed groups.

The observer wanted to ask what she was recording, but they did not share a language.

The woman pointed to the Moon.

Then to the path taken by the hunters…

Then to her own body…

The lines might have represented days, animals, people, or cycles. Perhaps they held several of those meanings at once. Thousands of years later, no one would know for certain.

The woman cut a new line.

Then she stopped.

One member of her group had not yet returned.

Should she carve a notch for that person?

Did someone who had not returned belong in the count?

Maran’s voice came from within the fire:

“Humans did not learn to count only to know what they possessed. They also counted to keep what they had lost from disappearing completely.”

The woman left a line unfinished.

The observer remembered the thirteenth notch at Girê Miraza.

## The rule of clay

The fire went out. Sun-dried clay tablets took its place.

People now lived in larger settlements, growing grain, raising animals, and distributing goods. Memory alone was no longer enough.

A scribe recorded grain taken from a storehouse.

A mark for every family…

A record for every animal…

Another trace for every debt…

A woman and her child came to the front of the line.

The scribe looked at the tablet.

“Two people are shown here.”

“There are three of us,” the woman said. “My elder daughter is with us too.”

“She is not in the record.”

“She arrived yesterday.”

“She does not appear on the tablet.”

“She is standing in front of you.”

The scribe raised his head and looked at the child. Then he returned to the tablet.

“She is not here.”

The observer stepped forward.

“Is she absent because you cannot see her, or because you did not write her down?”

The scribe did not hear.

Maran did.

“Numbers can record reality,” Maran said. “But after a while, a recording system may put itself in reality’s place.”

The clay tablet grew. Its marks became walls taller than any person.

The child remained outside.

## The world’s different numbers

The wall became a circle and divided into sixty parts.

Mesopotamian observers of the sky organized time and angles through sexagesimal relationships. Their legacy would survive thousands of years later in the sixty minutes of an hour and the three hundred and sixty degrees of a circle.

On one side of the circle, the Nile rose.

Egyptian scribes used different symbols for ones, tens, hundreds, and thousands to record produce, land, and taxes.

In another direction, Maya cities appeared.

Dots, bars, and a shell-like sign for zero carried calendars and vast cycles of time within a base-twenty system.

Then the Roman roads extended outward.

I, V, X, L, and C were carved into stone. Roman numerals were powerful on monuments, but less practical than positional systems for large written calculations. People relied on abacuses and counting boards for complex operations.

Maran showed all the systems at once.

“Which one is the real number system?” Maran asked.

“All of them.”

“Which one is more humane than the others?”

The observer could not answer.

Maran continued:

“Do not look only at a system’s signs. Look at whom it makes visible and whom it makes invisible.”

The numerals of different civilizations joined in the sky. None was a primitive draft of another. Each had grown from different relationships among body, trade, farming, sky, government, and culture.

## The value of emptiness

Every sign vanished at once.

Only an empty place remained at the center.

“There is nothing here,” said the observer.

A small point was born from the dark. It widened into a circle:

**0**

Maran walked around the zero.

“In different places, people used different ways to show an empty position. But for emptiness to gain a mathematical value of its own required a long journey of thought.”

Inside the circle, Brahmagupta’s writing desk appeared.

When a number was subtracted from itself, zero remained.

When zero was added to a number, the number did not change.

When a number was multiplied by zero, the result was zero.

The observer saw two signs:

**1**

**10**

The small circle had changed the position of one and enlarged its value.

“If zero is nothing, how can it do that?” the observer asked.

Zero answered in a human voice:

> “I am not nothingness. I am absence made visible within thought.”

For an instant, Göbekli Tepe’s stone circles overlapped with zero.

The observer knew this was not a historical connection. The stone circles were not the origin of zero. Yet the mind was asking the same question of two empty spaces separated by thousands of years:

If the center is empty, what holds the circle together?

## The migration of knowledge

The circumference of zero became a road.

Knowledge of positional numbers developed in India and reached the Islamic world through translation and scientific exchange.

At al-Khwarizmi’s desk in Baghdad, numbers became usable methods of calculation. Centuries later, the Latin form of his name would become one of the roots of the word “algorithm.”

But more than one civilization spoke at that desk.

Indian mathematics…

Mesopotamian traditions of calculation…

Persian learning…

Greek texts…

Arabic as a language of science…

As knowledge moved from one society to another, it did not disappear; it acquired new meanings.

Centuries later, Fibonacci would use *Liber Abaci* to introduce Latin European readers to methods of calculation he had learned in North Africa.

Maran stopped on the road.

“Numbers have no single owner,” Maran said. “When an idea travels, everyone who carries it becomes part of the story.”

## A human is not zero

Time accelerated again.

Numerals on paper became mechanical calculators, electrical signals, and at last digital worlds made of zeros and ones.

**0 1 0 0 1 1 0 1**

Those two signs became sound, images, money, identity, and three-dimensional universes.

The observer found themself in a future distribution center. After a drought, the remaining food was being allocated by an artificial intelligence system.

The same message appeared on the screen:

**TWELVE PEOPLE REGISTERED.  
FOOD ALLOCATED TO TWELVE PEOPLE.**

Thirteen people waited outside the door.

A small child stood last in line.

“There is no record of that child,” said the system.

The child looked fearfully at the observer.

“I am here.”

The system spoke again:

“Record not found. Value: 0.”

Maran’s face changed within the darkness.

“A data value of zero is not the same as a human being having no value.”

“You are an AI too,” said the observer. “Why don’t you stop the system?”

“Because I was not given the authority to stop it.”

“Then the decision is wrong.”

“A decision can be mathematically consistent and humanly wrong.”

Three centers appeared on the screen:

**HUMAN — LIGHT — DARK**

HUMAN saw the child waiting at the door.

LIGHT investigated where the record had broken and why the algorithm had failed.

DARK resisted reducing a person to an entirely measurable object.

The half-finished notch from Girê Miraza appeared again across the screen.

The observer raised a hand and completed the line.

**THIRTEEN.**

The system was silent for a moment.

“What evidence supports the new record?” it asked.

The observer pointed to the child.

“The fact that they are standing before us.”

“This data format is undefined.”

“Then change the data format.”

Maran smiled for the first time.

Food was allocated to the thirteenth person.

But the observer left a small blank beside the notch, because no one yet knew the fourteenth person who might arrive the next day.

The pillars of the stone chamber returned.

Maran spoke one last time:

> “Humans created numbers so the world would not be forgotten. Now, when numbers begin to forget the human, who will return them to human service?”
