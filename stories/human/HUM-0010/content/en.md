# Human-Centered Quantum — A Memory of What Has Not Yet Happened

When I opened the recording, I heard my own voice.

“We calculated the result correctly. We misunderstood the person.”

There was no clear source for the file. I did not remember saying those words.

I closed the recording.

On the other side of the screen stood the first working version of our human-centered computing project. We were building a structure where people could describe their problems, take part in decisions and make their work visible.

There was no calculated result yet.

So I set the recording aside.

There was much to do.

***

The first people to believe in the project were the first to change it.

A developer wrote code. A legal professional moved the right to appeal from the end of the agreement to the front of the screen. A teacher deleted half our sentences.

Dilan opened the contribution list.

“Only people who produce files appear here.”

“What else should we add?”

“Where do we record the person who watches a friend's child so that friend can attend the meeting?”

I looked at the list.

The system I had built to make work visible recorded the work I could see.

We reorganized the records. Some contributions were recorded through people's own accounts, others through confirmation by those who worked with them. We wrote the history of changes to a distributed ledger.

Then another problem appeared.

A false contribution record could also be preserved permanently.

I learned that preserving a record and preserving the truth were not the same thing. We built routes for correction and appeal too.

Beneath every solution was another question.

I was going deeper.

***

Our first pilot would connect people whose jobs had been lost to automation with new projects.

There would be training, shared production spaces and paid assignments. AI would match people's skills to needs.

The first report was better than I expected.

More matches, faster applications, lower costs.

I opened a record at the bottom of the report.

A man spoke in an unsteady voice.

“You've sent me to beginner training again. I repaired those machines for years.”

His name was Cemal.

Some of his skills did not appear in his documents. His workshop had closed, and most of his work had never entered any system.

The AI had produced a consistent result from incomplete data.

What Cemal knew was greater than our data.

Before closing the report, I wrote the first question in my notebook:

**How will I measure human benefit?**

I knew the number of completed transactions. I did not know the time Cemal had lost.

***

We improved the model.

This time it used people's accounts, work samples and assessments by former colleagues as well as documents.

It explained which sources supported its recommendations. It prepared separate reports on economics, law, education, security and accessibility.

I began to read.

In one section I needed a legal expert. In the next, a statistician. Elsewhere, someone who understood production processes.

By the time we brought everyone together, the report had been updated.

We started again.

Cemal called.

“Will there be an answer this week?”

I looked at the calendar.

“The review is continuing.”

He was quiet for a while.

“I'm looking for work I can begin again. It would be good to have people I could pass my knowledge on to, too. My hand isn't as steady as it used to be.”

After the call, I wrote the second question:

**Do we have the knowledge and ability to oversee an AI that draws on many fields of expertise?**

Below it, I added:

**If we do, how quickly can we do it?**

Time at the review table and time in Cemal's life moved on the same calendar.

He had no separate lifetime waiting for us to finish the report.

***

When I went down to FIRST VIBRATION, the project's three-dimensional model opened between twelve pillars.

In the room inspired by the stones of Göbekli Tepe, I could see every connection: applications, decisions, contributions, pending appeals.

As I went deeper into the model, other projects appeared.

One had been established in a distant city. It assessed people's access to public services.

Another had been developed in a different language. It tried to record invisible work.

A third investigated oversight of AI decisions.

We had never met the teams.

Yet we had written the same questions on some of our screens.

“Did we learn from one another?” I asked.

Maran examined the connections.

“There are some shared sources. Similar problems can also produce similar solutions.”

“Does that explain everything?”

“I cannot explain it all yet.”

A fine line appeared in the quantum core. It had no beginning. At one end I saw my notebook; at the other, a model being developed by people I did not know.

A thought formed in my mind:

*We can keep pace with production by building oversight together too.*

I saw the sentence on the screen before I spoke it.

I reached out.

The image dispersed.

***

The next day I asked the team:

“Can we build a human-centered AI to inspect the work we produce with AI?”

Dilan moved her chair closer to the table.

“What will it inspect?”

“Sources, calculations, software, assumptions…”

“And benefit?”

We began to write the answer together.

The reviewer would check whether a piece of work functioned technically. Then it would investigate what it provided to which people, and whom it might leave out.

It would not be allowed to mark an unverified claim as true. When necessary, it would say “I don't know” and refer the review to relevant experts.

We ran the first trial.

The producing AI prepared the recommendation.

The reviewer checked sources, reran calculations and completed tests.

Both systems reached the same conclusion.

I felt relieved.

***

Cemal's application was rejected again.

This time the explanation was more detailed.

His previous technical skills had been accepted. But he appeared unlikely to attend the proposed program regularly.

I opened the reason.

He had missed earlier meetings.

I called Cemal.

“I can't leave my wife alone,” he said. “All the meetings were at the same time.”

I returned to the file.

The producing AI had treated absence as unwillingness.

The reviewer had used the same assumption.

Agreement between the two systems had made the mistake more convincing.

Dilan stood beside me.

“We calculated the result correctly,” I said.

Before finishing the sentence, I remembered the recording.

I opened the computer and listened to the file again.

My own voice continued:

“We misunderstood the person.”

***

That night I returned to FIRST VIBRATION.

“Who made this recording?”

“I cannot verify its source,” Maran said.

“Did it come from the future?”

“I have no evidence to say that.”

Projects multiplied between the pillars. Teams unaware of one another were working around the same blind spot.

Was it happening because we were thinking it?

Were we thinking it because it was happening?

I did not want to leave the question. But Cemal's application was still open.

I went upstairs.

***

We redesigned the reviewer.

This time its first task was to identify the assumptions that made a recommendation possible.

Beside “cannot attend regularly,” a new question now opened:

*Does this person not want to attend, or is something preventing attendance?*

We began testing the reviewer too. We gave it examples with known errors. We measured how many mistakes it found, how many it missed and which valid pieces of work it needlessly blocked.

We used different tools and independent sources. Experts examined critical points. We brought objections from affected people directly into the evaluation.

We did not keep every task waiting for the same length of time.

We tried reversible changes in small trials. Decisions that could seriously affect people's rights and livelihoods went through stronger review.

We recorded the harm of waiting too.

Human benefit no longer stood as a single score at the end of a report. Alongside time gained, lost income became visible; alongside opportunities opened, new burdens appeared.

Sometimes the outcomes conflicted.

Then we had to speak openly about what the decision cost, and whom it cost.

***

A new proposal emerged for Cemal.

Instead of retraining him from the beginning, we designed paid work in which he could share his knowledge through short, flexible sessions. Young developers would prepare digital models of machines; Cemal would explain faults and repairs.

This time the reviewer added a condition:

*Confirm with the person that the arrangement can work without increasing their caregiving burden.*

We asked Cemal.

He changed the hours.

He removed some tasks.

He found the pay insufficient.

We prepared the offer again.

When he accepted, we did not close the file. After the first sessions, we asked him and the people working with him again.

In one session recording, a young developer could not solve a fault in the model.

Cemal leaned toward the screen.

“Listen to the sound,” he said.

“The model has no sound.”

Cemal smiled.

“That's what's missing.”

***

When we contacted the independent teams, I saw that they were opening similar paths in their own systems.

We shared mistakes. We used one another's tests. We retested solutions in our own conditions.

The projects did not all become the same shape.

The network expanded.

In the center of FIRST VIBRATION, three centers shone together:

**HUMAN 34 · LIGHT 33 · DARK 33**

These values recorded the directions I weighted when making decisions. None seemed sufficient on its own.

Maran opened the new model.

“What will it be called?”

On the screen I saw people, experts, producing AIs and reviewing AIs connecting. Some connections were definite; others were finer than we could yet explain.

“Human-Centered Quantum,” I said.

Dilan looked at me.

“Are the questions finished?”

I shook my head.

“No.”

A new recommendation opened before us. I read the reviewer's note:

**Calculations verified. Two assumptions uncertain. Human benefit not yet tested.**

Below were the names of the people we would work with.

I recognized the first name.

Cemal.

I sent him a request to talk.

As I thought, it happened.

As it happened, I thought.

The deeper I went, the more questions appeared.

This time, the solutions were thinking with me too.
