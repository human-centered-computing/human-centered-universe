# The Clock

## Eight

The first thing I learned was not a rule. It was a time.

Eight.

At eight, the doors opened. At eight, names became visible. At eight, the day began whether the body was ready or not.

I arrived once at eight minutes past.

Nobody shouted.

That was worse.

Someone looked at the clock, then at me.

“You are late.”

I wanted to explain that the work had not begun without me.

He did not ask.

## Twelve

At twelve, the rhythm changed.

People stood, moved, ate, returned.

For a short while nobody asked what had been completed.

Then the clock moved again.

The pause ended because the clock said it had ended.

## Five

At five, people began to leave.

I watched them.

Some hurried. Some remained seated.

The ones who stayed were sometimes praised.

“Dedicated,” someone said.

I wondered whether dedication could be measured by remaining after the hour had ended.

Years later, I understood that the clock did not merely tell us when to arrive and leave.

It taught us to divide ourselves into permitted pieces.

## The Clock Inside

After a while, nobody needed to tell us the time.

We knew.

I woke before the alarm.

I hurried without being late.

I felt guilty when I stopped.

One evening the clock on the wall stopped.

Nobody noticed for several minutes.

We continued working.

That was when I understood.

The clock had already moved inside us.

The supervisor no longer needed to point at it.

Neither did the rules.

We carried the schedule ourselves.

And perhaps that was the most efficient form of obedience:
not being watched,
but watching ourselves.
