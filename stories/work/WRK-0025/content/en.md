# Silence

The sound of the room had not changed. My voice had diminished. The moment I saw a question in a file and did not say it, I realized that this was not just between me and the supervisor.

I noticed that I had stopped distinguishing certain sounds in the room.

I heard the door opening.

Pull out the chairs.

Turning the papers over.

The operation of the printer.

That someone was walking fast down the corridor.

These were all the same.

What really changed was the things talked about.

In the past, when I saw a problem in a file, I could not help but mention it.

I would say that the number in one row does not match another number.

I would ask why a transaction was repeated.

I used to wonder where the result of a document was.

Sometimes I thought a sentence should have been written more clearly.

Then one day I saw the same sentence in three different places.

Nobody said anything.

I didn't say either.

In the first days of keeping silent, I thought I was smart. Why would I waste hours and hours on an idea if the supervisor wasn't listening anyway? Then I saw a wrong result in the file that came to my desk and I didn't even think about it until the end. That's what really scared me. I could get used to having my words blocked; But what a loss it was to have an idea hidden from me at birth? In the evening I opened a blank page. I didn't know the solution. I just wrote this sentence: “In whose life will this conclusion be confirmed?” The page was still on my desk the next day.

I closed the file.

I received another file.

After a while I realized it got easier.

One morning, I put a clean sheet of paper on my desk, just like the day I first arrived. With my old habit, I would write about a problem and its possible solution. I held the pen. First, I thought about the chief's face, then who would be present at the meeting, whose sentence would be considered to belong, and who would give an explanation if it turned out to be wrong. No words had made it to the middle of the page. These were all reasonable questions; yet none of them were of any help to the applicant. Even to say something to myself, I was asking permission from the invisible audience in the room.

The man at the next table looked at my page. “It's empty,” he said. "I am thinking." “You used to write while thinking.” I'm surprised he remembers this. He, the man who quietly performed every task, had seen me change. “Why don't you write?” I asked. “I write in my own notebook,” he said. He didn't open his drawer. I didn't ask why he didn't open it. Between us stood an invisible mass of ideas we could not say with confidence. We turned off the computers at five o'clock. Upon exit, the device registered that we were both working full time.

When he said something, he was asked who said it first.

Then why he said it.

Then whether it has been said before or not.

Then whether it is necessary or not.

Finally, whether it will be written to the relevant part of the file.

Sometimes a sentence was shorter before it was said.

After saying it, it got longer.

One day, a file arrived on my desk.

There was a small note on it in red pen:

It will be re-evaluated.

I opened the file.

Everything was in its place.

There was an application.

There were eclairs.

There was control.

There was a signature.

There were previous reviews.

At the end it said the same thing:

It will be re-evaluated.

The person at the next table looked up.

The person at the next desk asked twice whether something was wrong. I shook my head as if nothing were, and said no more.

He looked at me for a few seconds.

Then he turned to the screen in front of him.

So I left the file to the relevant place.

That day, for the first time, I had something to say but chose not to say it.

Then it became a habit.

A question was being asked at the meeting.

Even though I knew the answer, I was waiting.

Someone else was answering.

Sometimes he gave the wrong answer.

I was waiting again.

There was something missing in a file.

I was taking the pen in my hand.

Then I left it back.

There were times when I didn't understand what the numbers showed in a report.

“What does this number tell us?” I could ask.

I wasn't asking.

At the end of one file it said Completed.

I was wondering what the result was.

I was looking and passing by.

I continued working at the table.

One lunch break, someone sat across from me.

“You used to talk a lot.”

"Really?"

"Yes."

He stirred his tea.

“You're not suggesting anything anymore.”

“Not necessary.”

“How do you know?”

I didn't answer.

He took a sip of his tea.

“Something has changed.”

“My job is the same.”

“I'm not saying that.”

He got up from the table.

So I looked after him for a while.

That afternoon I found a small piece of paper on my desk.

There was only one sentence on it:

Why doesn't anyone say anything?

I couldn't understand who wrote it.

I turned the paper over.

There was nothing behind it.

I showed it to the person next to me.

“Did you write it?”

"No."

"Do you know?"

He nodded.

I left the paper on the table again.

A similar question arose at the afternoon meeting.

“Why is this done this way?”

Everyone in the room fell silent.

Someone looked at the file.

Someone to the computer.

One of them is on the paper in front of him.

Then the chief spoke:

“This is the way.”

I've heard the sentence many times before.

This time, hearing it from someone else's mouth, he stayed in the room a little longer.

The meeting is over.

I went out into the corridor.

The new person I was working with that day caught up with me.

"Can I ask something?"

"Of course."

“Why didn't anyone answer just now?”

I continued walking.

"I don't know."

“You too?”

I didn't answer.

After a while:

“You used to ask,” he said.

This time I stopped.

"Yes."

“What happened next?”

I looked at the window at the end of the corridor.

People were passing by outside.

“Some questions were not answered.”

"Later?"

“I didn't ask again until after a while.”

“Who told you not to ask?”

"Noone."

My voice lowered as I said this.

The new person looked at the file in his hand.

Then:

“I'll ask anyway,” he said.

I turned my head.

“What are you going to ask?”

“Will anything change?”

I didn't answer.

He opened the file.

There was a short suggestion in it.

An empty section below:

Why?

“I wrote this,” he said.

"From where?"

“I want something to change.”

“What will change?”

“I don't know yet.”

I looked at the file.

“Then write that down first.”

“What should I write?”

I thought for a while.

“The result you were waiting for.”

He took the pen in his hand.

He wrote a few words underneath.

Then he handed me the file.

Waiting time will be shorter.

He thought for a while longer.

He added below:

The applicant will know what to expect.

The sentences were flawed.

But for the first time I could see what he wanted.

“Let's ask this,” I said.

“From whom?”

"I don't know."

We both fell silent.

This time the silence came in a different way.

We did not close the file.

The next day, another paper arrived on my desk.

There was only one question on it:

Who knows the things we don't say?

There was an empty space underneath.

I took the pen.

I kept it for a while.

Then I didn't write anything.

I gave the paper to the new person.

“Then we leave it blank,” he said.

"Now."

“Can we fill it up later?”

"Maybe."

He took the paper to his desk.

So I returned to my own file.

After a while, a voice was heard inside the room.

"Can I ask something?"

I looked up.

This time the sound wasn't coming from my desk.

I saw that no one answered.

I pulled my chair back.

I waited a moment.

Then I stood up.

“Yes,” I said.

"Sir."
