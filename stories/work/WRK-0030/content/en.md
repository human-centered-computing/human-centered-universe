# Visible

We knew who had submitted the new application. But I didn't want to validate that person's expected outcome based solely on the institution's own record.

A new file arrived on my desk this morning.

The time clock recorded my name again that morning. In the afternoon, my departure time would appear on the same screen. That was still the measure of a day. But on the first page of the file on my desk, there was a new question: Who will verify the result? I knew that this question alone would not save anyone. Unless people's voices entered there, the gap could still be closed with a stamp. I opened the file; First I needed to know what the applicant expected.

There was only the file number on the cover.

A quick note inside:

Providing information about the outcome of the application.

There was a date beneath it.

I opened the file.

There was a sentence on the first page.

A short sentence.

It was similar to the sentences I read in other files.

It wasn't the same.

The new person I was working with that day came to my desk.

“Who wrote this?”

"I don't know."

He looked at the file.

“Shall we find out?”

“Let's understand what it does first.”

We started reading the file.

The application was received.

It was noticed that one document was missing.

The document was completed.

Someone else reviewed it.

A correction has been made.

Then the file was sent to another table.

“What is supposed to happen here?” he asked.

I looked at the first page.

“The applicant knows what it is.”

“So what will happen?”

“We need to find this out.”

We did not move the file from table to table.

First we called by phone.

We asked the applicant what he expected.

He explained.

The newcomer listened.

This time he took notes.

“He's said that before,” he said.

“It's not in the file.”

“There is now.”

He put the note into the file.

He wrote his name underneath.

Then he stopped.

“Did you add it too?”

"Not yet."

"What have you done?"

I showed him the first page of the file.

“I changed the wording here.”

“Did you write that?”

"Yes."

“Then write.”

I took the pen.

I wrote my name next to the change I made.

We closed the file.

The file came back in the afternoon.

Someone else had worked on it.

One sentence had changed.

A document was added.

A date had been corrected.

Newcomer:

“Who did these?”

We looked at the file.

Some were obvious.

Some appeared in the change history.

There was no name next to one of them.

“Who did this?”

"I don't know."

“Shall we find it?”

We turned on the computer.

We looked at the change history.

There was a dated entry.

There was a time stamp.

But there was no username.

We looked at the screen for a while.

“What do we write here?”

“We don't know.”

“Should we leave it blank?”

“Let's leave it at that.”

A small note fell next to it:

Source could not be verified.

We closed the file.

In the evening the applicant came again.

We put the file in front of him.

“We came to inform you about the result.”

He listened.

He asked something.

We answered.

He thought for a while.

“Yes,” he said.

“This is what I was looking for.”

The newcomer looked at me.

He opened the last page.

He wrote down the date.

Then:

“Can you confirm this?” he asked.

The man nodded.

"Yes."

The newcomer wrote.

The result is confirmed.

The man looked at the file.

He saw the sentence on the first page.

“When was this sentence written?”

“At the very beginning.”

“Who wrote it?”

I was silent for a moment.

"I."

The man nodded.

“Well thought out.”

Then he closed the file.

“Is my job done?”

"Yes."

This time I was looking at the last page while saying this word.

After the man left, the new person opened the file again.

“Something is missing.”

"Where?"

He showed the first page.

“Your name is here.”

"Yes."

“There are other names on the following pages.”

"Yes."

“There are no resources anywhere.”

"Yes."

“So who did it?”

I looked at the file.

“One person didn't.”

"I know."

“Whatever happened appears on the pages.”

The newcomer turned to the first page.

Then to the last page.

“Who completed this file?”

"I don't know."

“You too?”

"Yes."

We remained silent for a while.

Then:

“It's done together,” he said.

I nodded.

We did not write this sentence in the file.

When the chief walked in, he had a monthly chart in his hand. He covered the two pillars with his finger; Only hours remained in the open.

“You two have been here for the same amount of time,” he said. “Which of you worked harder?”

The newcomer opened the file that the applicant had just closed. "I listened to the applicant. He corrected the previously lost sentence. Someone else completed the document. Another hand found the wrong date. The man confirmed the result."

“I asked for a name,” said the chief.

“The job is not just a name,” I said. “There is none of this on the timetable.”

The chief looked at the notes on the edge of the file. “What if everyone writes this in his own favor?”

“It won't be right in itself,” said the newcomer. "The employee will see what he has done, other contributors will verify his share. The person who reaches the result will be able to tell what happened to him. We will not write a name next to a source we do not know."

“What if there is no result?”

This time I had a hard time answering. A person who worked all day to prevent a mistake would sometimes have no results to show for it.

“That too will be written,” I said. "The mistake prevented, the method tried, why no results were achieved. If we measure a person only by the number of successful files, no one will touch the difficult work."

The chief opened the chart again. “So, should we delete the check-in and check-out times?”

The newcomer looked up. "Let's not delete it. We still need a record to show our overtime and our right to rest. But the clock should not replace the work we do."

The chief flipped through the file for a while. Lastly, he focused on the change with my name on it.

“Who came up with this idea?” he asked.

“He heard what the applicant said,” I said. "We changed the question together. He kept the record. If you close it with only one person's name, we will return to our first mistake."

The chief took out a blank page. He wrote “Work Done” on it. We opened other headings next to it: "Who contributed?", "What changed?", "To whom did it reach?", "How was it verified?" We left another blank: “Unknown yet.”

The newcomer wrote three more questions at the top of the page. He read each one aloud before raising his hand:

“What has changed for humans?”

“How do we know this?”

“What else can we do better?”

On the third question, the chief looked at the pen. “Who will write this if we fail?”

“Us again,” I said. “If we delete the failed attempt, you will have someone else do the same job again.”

Three charts were on the table by the door. The same files were arranged in three separate rows. The newcomer looked at them.

“Which of these reached the applicant?”

The chief did not answer. He took the charts; All three had their own instructions on them. He wrote “Unnecessary repetition” on the margin of the page. We did not remove the names of those who made those repetitions; The one who gave the instruction wrote his own name on the same line for the first time.

“So will you watch me all day?” asked the newcomer. For the first time there was fear in his voice.

“We will verify your work together,” I said. "We will not record your every move. The applicant's private life and the contribution written in your name will only be disclosed to the person who needs to see it."

“What if I can't figure it out?”

“You write down what you tried and what you learned. If it turns out wrong, you add your correction instead of secretly changing the old line.”

The new sheet on the table did not look like a list of achievements. In its unfinished language, it asked whether the work mattered to a human life, how we knew that, and what we could change next time.

“Let's try this on this file first,” said the chief.

The newcomer did not fill the page immediately. First he looked at the notes of those who touched the file; He did not approve on behalf of those who were not with them. I read my own sentence, corrected it, and only then agreed to keep my name.

The time sheet remained in the supervisor’s hand. It would be needed for pay and overtime. The other sheet on the table described what we had done here, beyond the mere fact that we had been present.

I read the file again before leaving for the night.

First sentence.

Next change.

Attached document.

Correction made.

Line whose source cannot be verified.

Applicant's response.

Conclusion.

Verification.

There was a different hand on every page.

Name in some places.

History in some places.

Just a trace in some places.

There was a place for signature at the end of the file.

I took the pen.

I waited a moment.

Then I signed.

Newcomer:

“We can close it now.”

I closed the file.

We went out into the corridor.

Another file was sitting on a table.

Its cover was open.

The newcomer stopped.

“Should we look at this?”

I looked at the file.

"Tomorrow."

"Ok."

We continued walking.

At the end of the corridor, someone was handing a file to a newcomer.

“You will follow this.”

The new person took the file.

He looked at the cover for a while.

Then:

“What is supposed to happen in the end?” he asked.

The other person paused before answering.

"I don't know."

“Then let's find that out first.”

We continued walking.

The sound of pages being turned came from behind us.

A file was opened.

A question was asked.

Someone was looking for answers.

I didn't look back.

The door was open.

It was evening outside.

The person next to me:

“See you tomorrow,” he said.

"See you later."

I went out.

This time I didn't check if the door was closed behind me.
