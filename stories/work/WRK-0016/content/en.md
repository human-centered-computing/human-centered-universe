# WRK-0016 — What Becomes Visible

Dostoevsky's *Notes from a Dead House* came back to me after many years.

I do not know why I remembered it that day.

Perhaps it was because of the paper in front of me.

Perhaps, while trying to understand what the task before me actually meant, a thought from that old book had returned.

Dostoevsky understood that merely making a person work hard was not enough. There was another way to break a person: make them feel that what they were doing was completely meaningless.

To do a task.

Then do the same task again.

Then again.

Eventually, a person feels that not only the body is tired, but the work itself has become tired.

I closed the book.

I looked at the paper in front of me.

Then, for the first time, I asked myself plainly:

**What was the difference between the work I was doing and that?**

The answer was not easy.

Because nobody had put chains on me.

Nobody had forced me into the chair.

Every morning I came by myself.

I scanned my card myself.

I sat at my desk myself.

And when evening came, I left by myself.

So what was the difference?

They worked too.

So did I.

Some of their tasks led nowhere.

Some of mine did too.

Sometimes the result of their work could not be seen.

The result of mine often disappeared inside another file.

Their day ended, and the same work began again the next day.

So did mine.

I thought for a while.

Perhaps the difference was not in the door.

Perhaps it was in the relationship between a person and what they made.

That day they showed us something new.

They brought us into the same room.

There were still desks.

There were still papers.

There was still someone in charge.

But this time, the paper placed in front of us did not contain only the task.

At the top was another heading:

**Result.**

Under it was a clear description of what should exist when the work was finished.

I stared at it.

I asked the man beside me:

“What is this?”

“I don't know.”

The supervisor came closer.

“You have never done this before.”

“What are we supposed to do?”

“Reach the result.”

“How?”

“You will find that out.”

I had never heard an answer like that.

For years, the task came first, and then we were told how to perform it.

Now we knew where we had to arrive.

How we would get there was left to us.

At first nobody moved.

Then someone offered an idea.

Someone else thought the idea was incomplete.

A third person suggested another way.

I combined the two.

Someone else found a mistake in what we had made.

As the work progressed, it became difficult to separate who had done what.

But this time, becoming intertwined did not mean disappearing.

The system recorded every contribution separately.

An idea.

A change.

A correction.

An implementation.

A complementary contribution.

When the result appeared, the screen showed more than the result.

There were names beneath it.

Mine.

The name of the man beside me.

The name of the person who had first offered the idea.

The name of the person who had found the error.

We had not erased one another.

For one person to be visible, another did not have to disappear.

I looked at the screen for a long time.

I thought about the work I had done over the years.

Ideas I had once offered.

Suggestions I had forgotten because nobody used them.

Projects that continued under someone else's name.

Files that were used without anyone knowing who had made them.

Work that produced results without anyone knowing whose effort had produced them.

Then I understood:

**My work had not disappeared.**

**My contribution had become invisible.**

Perhaps what had exhausted me for years was not the weight of the work.

Perhaps the deeper exhaustion was the breaking of the connection between a person and what they had made.

The work continues.

The file continues.

The result continues.

But if the person's name, contribution, and trace gradually disappear, eventually the person becomes a stranger even to their own work.

I now saw another difference between the meaningless tasks Dostoevsky described and the work I had done.

A task can be difficult.

It can take a long time.

It can exhaust a person.

But if a person knows why the work is being done, can see its result, and knows that their own effort has a place in that result, then the work is not merely a punishment.

But if a person is constantly told:

“Do this,”

without being told why,

without being shown the result,

without having their contribution recorded,

while their idea passes into someone else's name,

and everything they do becomes merely a number,

then the name of the activity may remain work.

But its meaning inside the person changes.

That day I asked my question again:

**What is the difference between work and punishment?**

I already knew the answer was not in the door.

Nor in the clock.

Nor in the shape of the desk.

Perhaps the answer was this:

**Can a person see a trace of themselves in the result of what they have done?**

For the first time, I could see it in the new system.

We had started something.

We had changed it together.

We had completed it together.

And when the result appeared, nobody had taken another person's contribution for themselves.

I did not think it was something extraordinary.

I thought it was what should have been ordinary.

People creating something together without erasing one another while they create it.

For the first time, we saw that work could be different.

Nobody told us that we were free.

Nobody tore down the walls.

The doors were the same.

The clock still showed the same hour.

But the meaning of what we were doing had changed.

As I left that evening, I looked at the door.

As I had on the first day, I did not wonder whether it was locked.

For the first time, I thought about something else:

**Perhaps making a person work is one thing, and valuing that person's work is another.**

I walked out.

The next morning I came again.

There was a new task in front of me.

But it was not only a task.

There was a result to reach.

And my contribution would have a place in that result.

Perhaps this was where the difference between work and punishment began.

**When a person can see the result of what they have done and find their own contribution inside that result.**

I looked at the screen one last time.

My name was there.

Together with other names.

And for the first time, I was not troubled by that.

Because now I knew:

**Another person becoming visible did not mean that I had to become invisible.**

Perhaps human-centered work should begin here.

By not separating a person's labor from the person who gave it.
