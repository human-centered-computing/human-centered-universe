# Trace

A mark as small as a scratch on the table could be a reminder of labor that was not included in the file. However, attributing the trace that we do not know who left it to someone was not the same as understanding it.

While cleaning the table, I noticed a small scratch.

It was near the right corner.

It wasn't very deep.

I ran my finger over it.

We didn't know who made the scratch. My first thought was to write a name there. Then I thought that all those who took over the table might have left a mark on it. There was a difference between tracking down labor and finding an owner for it. Sometimes we would have to show the courage required to leave blank the areas we did not know in the files, even in the ones we thought we knew.

The new person I worked with that day:

“What happened?”

he asked.

"I don't know."

“Old?”

"Probably."

The table has been cleared.

It remained scratched.

After a while, we opened one of the old files.

There was a small mark on the edge of one page.

A line.

Two words next to it:

Look again.

Newcomer:

“What is this?”

"I don't know."

“Who wrote it?”

There were no initials next to the name.

He turned the page.

The next page had the same sign.

This time with:

There may be a problem here.

“Is this written by the same person?”

I looked.

"I think."

"Are you sure?"

"No."

He did not close the file.

We continued working on the file.

The newcomer changed one line.

Then he looked at his old self.

“Who changed this?”

“Previous person.”

"Why?"

"I don't know."

“So is the change correct?”

I looked at the file.

It was checked.

There was history next to it.

But it did not say why the change was made.

The newcomer opened the old line again.

“Let's not erase this.”

"Why?"

“Then we can forget why you changed.”

The old line remained below.

He focused on new information.

It seemed like both situations.

After a while we came across an old fix in another file.

Newcomer:

“Let's change this.”

he said.

“Change.”

“Shall it remain as it was?”

I looked at the file.

"Leave."

"Why?"

“We'll see later.”

Old information remains.

New information was written next to it.

It was still unclear who changed it and why.

That evening I found a photo in a file.

There was a table in the photo.

Files on it.

One glass.

A pen.

A small scratch on the right corner.

There was only a date on the back of the photo.

The new person took the photo into my hand.

“Is this the table?”

"Yes."

“Ours?”

“Old table.”

“Why did they take your photo?”

"I don't know."

He put the photo back in the folder.

The next day the former employee came.

He stopped when he saw us in the corridor.

The new person showed the file in his hand.

"Can I ask something?"

The man took the file.

He opened the page.

“Did you make that correction?”

He looked for a while.

"Yes."

"Why?"

The man looked at the line again.

“It wouldn't happen any other way here.”

Newcomer:

“That's how we do it now.”

he said.

The man nodded.

He gave the file back.

He would go.

Then he stopped.

“Did you find the photo?”

The newcomer was surprised.

“Photo?”

Man:

“I pulled the table.”

he said.

“There was a scratch.”

After the former employee left, we took out the photo again.

The newcomer looked at the photo of the table.

Then to the table next to us.

He leaned into the right corner.

He ran his finger over the line.

“Did you do this?”

"No."

“Then he is.”

I didn't answer.

He put the photo into the file.

He looked at the table.

The scratch was still there.
