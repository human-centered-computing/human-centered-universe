# Idea

All processing dates were in place in the application sitting on my desk; However, there was no line showing what had changed in the applicant's life. I started drawing a tracking chart even though no one wanted it. It was not yet clear on which hand the lines would start and where they would change.

It was difficult to understand where an idea started.

Sometimes it would come to mind.

Sometimes it was found on the edge of a file.

Sometimes it was said in the middle of a conversation.

Sometimes it would come back as someone else's sentence.

There was such a problem in one of the files on my desk.

I took the three-column painting home the night I prepared it. Just say "What happened?" in front of a line. In order to write, I opened past files and searched for the aftermath of the application. Every result I couldn't find was a person I had to look at again in the morning. There was no room to take this labor into account; Still, I believed the painting would work. For the first time there was something on my desk that I wasn't ordered to finish. I had almost forgotten what it felt like to commit to a task of one's own free will.

The files were not lost.

We could see where they were.

It was obvious who was on it.

It was clear to whom it was sent.

It was clear at which stage he was waiting.

But it didn't say what was at the end of the file.

A file arrived.

It had several dates on it.

First delivery.

Second check.

My third post.

Then another date.

Then a signature.

Next to the file was a small box:

Status: Completed.

I opened the file.

There were dozens of pages in it.

One transaction appeared on each page.

On the last page, there was only one question left:

The result?

The bottom was empty.

I asked the person who brought the file whether the application or merely its processing was finished. The processing was complete. The result was written nowhere.

“So how do we know?”

The man did not answer.

I put the file on my desk.

I took out a piece of paper.

I drew three columns.

To the first:

Where?

To the second:

Who has it?

To the third:

What happened?

The first two were easy to fill.

Table.

Unit.

Person.

History.

Situation.

The third column remained empty.

The person next to me looked at the paper.

"What are you doing?"

“I'm trying to understand something.”

"What?"

“We know where the file is.”

"Yes."

“We don't know what happened.”

“It's finally complete.”

“Is this the same thing?”

The man looked at the paper.

Then to the file.

"I don't know."

After a while, I started to see the same question in other files.

Some had results.

In some, it's just transaction.

In some cases, a new date instead of a result.

In some:

It was sent to the relevant unit.

In some:

It's about to be done.

In some:

Waiting.

On a file:

It has been concluded.

it said.

I opened it.

On the last page:

The result is awaited.

it said.

I closed the file.

One day, I brought up this issue at a meeting.

There were several people at the table.

Files in front of us.

Pen in someone's hand.

Computer in front of someone.

“We see what stage the file is at.”

I said.

"Later?"

“Then what?”

“What happened in the end.”

Someone in the room:

“It closes once it is concluded.”

he said.

“So what does concluding mean?”

“Get the job done.”

“What does it mean to be done?”

There was silence at the table.

Someone:

“The transaction is completed.”

he said.

“So what's changing?”

Nobody answered.

One person closed the file.

“It's not easy to put these into the system.”

he said.

“Put what?”

“Consequence.”

"Why?"

“The outcome of every job is not measured in the same way.”

This sentence was correct.

But it didn't fill the gap in front of me.

After the meeting, I collected my notes.

I reopened the file.

I looked at the first page.

I looked at the last page.

All movements in between were visible.

The file was not lost.

People worked.

There were signatures.

Checks were made.

But somewhere:

What has changed?

There was no answer to the question.

The next day the same file arrived on my desk.

This time there was another note next to him.

Suggestion: Add a result field.

I didn't know who the note belonged to.

It wasn't the paper I wrote on.

But the sentence was similar to what I was thinking.

I put the note in the file.

After a while someone came to me.

He had a paper in his hand.

“Did you say that?”

I looked at the paper.

On it, next to the movements of the file, there was another line:

expected result

“I suggested something like this.”

I said.

“Who said that?”

"I don't know."

The man left the paper on the table.

“Will it work?”

"Maybe."

“How will we understand?”

I looked at the paper.

“When you see the result.”

After a while, that line started to be used in some files.

Not in every file.

In some:

expected result

it said.

In some:

What will happen?

Some had nothing.

In a file just:

What will change?

It was written.

One day a woman came to my table.

He had an old file in his hand.

“I've said this idea before.”

he said.

He opened the file.

He took out a page.

There was a table at the top of the page showing where the files were located.

A few handwritten notes underneath.

“Who prepared this?”

“I told you.”

“You have no name here.”

"I know."

“So who did it?”

The woman lifted her shoulders.

“Someone did it.”

He handed me the page.

“It's nice to have the job done.”

he said.

“I just don't want the idea to get lost.”

I took note of the sentence.

Don't lose the idea.

I didn't write anything else underneath.

The same idea was discussed in a different form at a meeting.

This time, someone sitting on the other side of the table was telling the story.

He said it should be possible to see not only where the files are, but also what happened at the end.

Someone:

“Good idea.”

he said.

Another:

“We can try this.”

he said.

The speaker continued.

I listened silently.

What they said was similar to the sentences on the paper I had prepared before.

Some words were the same.

Some had changed.

No one in the meeting looked at me.

After the meeting, that person came to me.

“Do you want to add anything?”

he said.

"Yes."

"What?"

“What if we note that the idea has been discussed before?”

"How?"

I thought for a while.

"I don't know."

We opened a small section under the paper.

Idea:

The opposite side remained empty for a while.

One day the supervisor called me.

There was my old paper on his desk.

The edges were curled.

There were coffee stains in some places.

“Did you do this?”

"Yes."

He turned the paper over.

There was someone else's handwriting on the back.

“We use this.”

he said.

I looked at the paper.

The first lines were mine.

I didn't know who the next notes belonged to.

“Does it work?”

I asked.

The Chief squared his shoulders.

“In some files.”

He handed me the paper.

I bought.

I returned to my desk.

I took out the old pen from the drawer.

I wrote a sentence on the side of the paper:

Where does an idea begin?

I looked for a while.

I did not reply.

I opened the file.

There were movements on the first page.

The same question was still on the last page.

What will change?

I left the pen on the table.

I did not close the file.
